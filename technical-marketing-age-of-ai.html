A structural argument.

Clarified.

Technical Marketing in the Age of AI.

AI is compressing artifact production across every discipline that moves enterprise software through the market. Technical marketing does not contract under that pressure. It clarifies. The case for why the discipline expands when everything around it shrinks.

01 · The Condition

What is happening to the GTM stack

The Condition AI compression of GTM artifact production

The systematic absorption of structured content production across go-to-market functions by AI tooling. Affects drafting, summarization, formatting, and initial research compilation. Does not affect the editorial judgment that decides which artifact to produce, which claim to anchor it to, or whether that claim will survive contact with a technically capable buyer.

Something is moving through enterprise software go-to-market right now. It is not a threat. It is a compression. Every function that produces structured technical content for the sales motion is being operated on by AI tooling that absorbs the mechanical labor of production. Decks get drafted. Competitive summaries get generated. Deployment guides get assembled. The pipeline from raw product knowledge to formatted artifact is collapsing.

The compression is documented. Gartner projected that 40 percent of enterprise applications would be integrated with task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026, up from less than five percent in 2025.1 Salesforce reduced support headcount from nine thousand to approximately five thousand. Marc Benioff stated publicly that the company would hire no new software engineers in fiscal year 2026. Microsoft, Workday, Atlassian, and Oracle each cited AI efficiency as the structural rationale for headcount reduction across adjacent functions.2

The compression is real. The question is what it compresses and what it does not. The answer to that question determines whether technical marketing contracts with the rest of the GTM stack or occupies something different.

The compression is real. The question is what it compresses and what it does not.

02 · The Compression Layer

What AI actually absorbs

The work AI compresses is artifact production. The mechanical translation of existing knowledge into structured format. A language model given a product brief and a competitor's published positioning can produce a useful competitive summary in minutes. The same model, given a feature description and a reference architecture, can draft a technical slide. Given a pricing table and a set of published proof points, it can assemble a first-pass evaluation framework.

This is not nothing. Artifact production has consumed a substantial portion of the technical marketing role's time for as long as the role has existed. The TME who spent four hours building a competitive teardown deck now has tooling that produces a draft in forty minutes. The hours freed are real. The compression is net positive for the practitioner who understands how to use it.

What it does not compress is the judgment that decides which artifact to build, which claim to anchor it to, and whether the claim holds under the questioning it will face. A model can draft a competitive teardown. It cannot decide whether to lead with the architectural weakness or the pricing gap, because that decision depends on which competitor the prospect is currently evaluating, what the analyst who will see this deck actually cares about, and what the engineering team will actually stand behind if pushed. Those inputs do not live in a prompt. They live in the room.

The room is where technical marketing lives.

03 · The Trust Gap

Why technically capable buyers do not trust what AI produces

The enterprise software buyer most likely to evaluate a complex product is also the buyer most likely to distrust AI-generated content about it. This is not sentiment. It is data.

29%
of developers trusted the accuracy of AI tool output in 2025, down from 40% the year before. More developers actively distrust AI output (46%) than trust it.
Stack Overflow Developer Survey, 2025 · 49,000 respondents across 177 countries

Stack Overflow's 2025 Developer Survey found that trust in AI accuracy fell eleven percentage points in a single year, from forty percent to twenty-nine.3 When asked what they would still want from a human even if AI could do most coding tasks, seventy-five percent of developers said they would not trust AI answers. The number-one frustration, cited by forty-five percent of respondents, was dealing with AI solutions that were almost right but not quite.

Almost right is the precise failure mode that kills a technical sale. A principal engineer reviewing an architecture diagram that is almost right does not engage with the content. They disengage from the company. A CISO reviewing a security questionnaire that overpromises by one degree does not note the caveat. They flag the vendor. The tolerance for almost right in the rooms where technical marketing works is zero.

McKinsey's 2026 AI Trust Maturity Survey found that seventy-four percent of organizations identify inaccuracy as a highly relevant AI risk, and nearly two-thirds cite security and risk concerns as the top barrier to scaling agentic AI deployment.4 The CEO of Stack Overflow, speaking to McKinsey's technology practice, put the condition plainly: "Having high-quality, expert-verified knowledge inside organizations has become critical, not a nice-to-have."5

Expert-verified knowledge is a job description. It is the technical marketer's job description.

Almost right is the precise failure mode that kills a technical sale.

04 · The Favorable Position

Where technical marketing sits on the compression map

Article I of this volume introduced the function matrix: technical depth on one axis, commercial orientation on the other.6 The matrix identifies the disciplines that touch enterprise software GTM, and it places technical marketing alone in the quadrant that requires high scores on both. That position is relevant here for a different reason.

When AI compresses artifact production across the GTM stack, the disciplines most exposed are those that produce structured content as their primary output. Technical writing. First-pass documentation. Boilerplate competitive summaries. Standard sales decks. Those outputs were already mechanical. AI has now made them more efficiently mechanical. The practitioners who built their value on producing those outputs at volume are facing genuine compression.

The disciplines least exposed are those where the output is judgment under commercial pressure rather than structured content at volume. The disciplines where the question is not how to produce the artifact but which artifact to produce, which claim to stake, and whether the company can defend it when the analyst pushes back. That question requires holding the buyer's specific evaluation context, the company's commercial pressure, the engineering team's honest constraints, and the competitive landscape simultaneously. It cannot be usefully delegated to a model that has none of those inputs.

Figure 01. Disciplines plotted against AI exposure of artifact production and editorial leverage required. Technical marketing sits in the favorable quadrant: high AI exposure at the production layer, which accelerates it, combined with editorial leverage that cannot be compressed. The compression removes the mechanical burden. The judgment stays.

The compression does not threaten the favorable quadrant. It reveals it. When AI absorbs the production work that surrounded the judgment, the judgment becomes visible as the scarce resource. What was always the most valuable thing the role did becomes the explicit center of it.

05 · The Clarification

What the discipline becomes when the production layer lifts

The compression of artifact production does not remove work from the technical marketing role. It removes the part of the work that was never the role's highest-leverage contribution. The hours that went into first drafts, formatting, initial research compilation, and basic content assembly get absorbed by tooling. The hours that remain are the ones that determined whether the first draft was worth producing in the first place.

Which competitor to address. Which architectural claim is defensible and which overpromises by a degree the engineering team will not stand behind in a live briefing. Whether the demo should show the integration or the native capability, and why that choice changes depending on whether the audience is a Gartner analyst or a buying committee's principal engineer. That decision set has always been the role's real work. AI does not touch it.

The production layer lifts.
First drafts, research compilation, initial formatting, and structural assembly are absorbed by tooling. The technical marketer who understands this uses AI to produce artifacts in a fraction of the previous time. The hours freed are real. The output volume increases. The judgment that decides which output to produce does not change.
The judgment layer becomes explicit.
When production is no longer scarce, the scarcity shifts to the editorial decisions that govern production. Which claim. Which format. Which audience. Which competitor. Those decisions were always the role's highest-leverage contribution. They are now the role's most visible one, because the production work that obscured them has been absorbed.
The verification function grows.
Every AI-generated artifact that enters the technical sales motion requires a practitioner who can verify it. That the architecture claim is technically accurate. That the competitive comparison does not overstate. That the security questionnaire response will hold under a follow-up call from the buyer's security team. The more AI produces, the more essential the verification layer becomes. The technical marketer is that layer.
The accountability surface expands.
A technically capable buyer who encounters an AI-generated claim that proves inaccurate assigns accountability to the company, not the model. The technical marketer is the practitioner whose name is on the claim. That accountability is not delegatable. It is the role's permanent structural position in a GTM motion that increasingly relies on AI-generated content.

The more AI produces, the more essential the verification layer becomes.

06 · The Counterexample

What Boris Cherny's prediction gets right, and where it stops

The strongest version of the opposite argument does not come from a skeptic of AI. It comes from the person who built the tool doing the compressing. Boris Cherny, creator and head of Claude Code at Anthropic, has not written a line of code himself in more than six months.7 He has said the title "software engineer" could start to disappear this year, dissolving into something closer to "builder" as designers, product managers, and generalists start shipping code directly.7 If a title this established can dissolve, the question this article has to answer is whether the same thing happens to technical marketing.

Part of Cherny's argument is not in tension with the one made here. It is the same argument, applied one layer over. He is not saying engineers stop mattering. He is saying the mechanical layer of the work, the part that was always closest to production, stops requiring a dedicated title, while the judgment above it (what to build, for whom, and why) becomes the thing that actually distinguishes a practitioner. That is compression revealing judgment. This article's argument, restated in someone else's domain.

Compression revealing judgment is not a threat to this argument. It is the argument, made twice.

Where it stops is structural, not sentimental, but the honest version of the line is narrower than "engineering versus technical marketing." Cherny's own framework groups his team into five archetypes: Prototyper, Builder, Sweeper, Grower, Maintainer.8 Read that list closely. Every archetype describes a way of moving a product forward. None of them describes a way of being accountable to someone outside the company for a claim that person cannot independently verify in the room. That is not a gap unique to engineering versus marketing. It is a gap between two kinds of work that exist inside almost every technical discipline, including engineering itself: production work, which has a correctness standard a machine or a test suite can check, and attestation work, which has a correctness standard only a specific, named human can stand behind. Code either compiles, passes the suite, or survives production. A technical claim made to a buyer has no such oracle. The only check is whether a skeptical person, later, decides they were told the truth.

Engineering has its own attestation roles, and they are worth naming honestly rather than assuming away: the SRE whose name is on a postmortem, the security engineer who signs a compliance attestation, the release manager who owns a rollback call. None of Cherny's five archetypes covers that work either, and none of this article's argument implies it should dissolve. If anything, those roles are the engineering-side proof of the same point: attestation work is exactly what survives when production work compresses, wherever it sits. Technical marketing is not structurally separate from engineering on this axis. It is a discipline that happens to be almost entirely attestation work, with very little production layer to compress in the first place, which is why the compression this article describes reveals it so completely rather than partially.

The line is not engineering versus technical marketing. It is production work versus attestation work, and it runs through both.

Attestation work has no owner in Cherny's model, in either discipline.
Prototyper, Builder, Sweeper, Grower, Maintainer are all functions internal to building a product. None of them is the function that stands in front of a buyer, an analyst, or a regulator and answers for whether a specific technical claim is true, and none of them is the SRE signing a postmortem or the security engineer signing a compliance attestation either. That function does not disappear when the others merge, in engineering or in technical marketing. It becomes more visible, for the same reason argued in Section 05: production work recedes, and what is left is whoever is accountable.
This genre of prediction has a mixed track record.
"Citizen developer," "growth hacker," "full-stack marketer": each was framed at launch as evidence that specialist titles were becoming obsolete. Each did expand who could do a piece of the work. None of them eliminated the accountability-bearing specialist sitting behind the generalist tooling. A structural argument about title dissolution is worth taking seriously. It is not automatically right because the person making it built the tool in question.
Cherny's own number cuts against the strong reading of his claim.
In the same interview, asked whether there will be more or fewer engineers in three years, Cherny answered that the number of people writing code or directing agents to write it will be roughly a hundred times what it is today, even if most of them are not called engineers.7 That is not a story about the work disappearing. It is a story about the work multiplying while the title thins out, which is a different claim, and a more defensible one, than "the discipline goes away."

Practitioners inside the field have already pushed back on the "builder" framing for exactly this reason: engineers who review AI output, decide what actually ships, and keep the work grounded in reality do not disappear when the label changes, they become the reason the label still needs someone behind it.9 That is the same position this article takes about technical marketing, applied to the discipline next door.

So: if Cherny is right that titles built around production dissolve into generalist labels, technical marketing should expect the same pressure on the boundaries between TMM, PMM, and developer advocacy, because those boundaries were always drawn around production work more than attestation work. Titles have always been the least stable part of this discipline; Article II traces twelve of them across six decades. What Cherny's own framework does not have, in engineering or anywhere else, is a name for the function that owns a claim someone outside the company cannot verify for themselves. Technical marketing has always been closer to that function than to a production role, which is why it reads as more resistant to this pressure than engineering does, not because it sits in some separate category. Building an equivalent attestation function from scratch, under a title that has not caught up yet, is exactly what Section 08 already documents happening at AI companies today. The title question stays genuinely open. The attestation question does not.

07 · The Practitioner's Posture

What this requires in practice

The technical marketer who understands this transition does not resist AI tooling. They use it to accelerate the production layer while protecting their editorial authority over what the production layer produces. The draft that used to take four hours now takes forty minutes. The judgment about whether the draft is accurate, whether the claim holds, whether the framing serves the buyer's actual evaluation question: that judgment takes exactly as long as it always did, because it requires the same inputs it always required.

The transition asks two things of the practitioner. First, enough comfort with AI tooling to use it without deferring to it. The practitioner who defers to the model's framing rather than evaluating it against the product's actual engineering reality is producing AI-assisted content that will fail under buyer pressure. The model's framing is a starting point. The practitioner's judgment is the edit. Second, clarity about which work to protect. The editorial decisions, which claim, which competitor, which audience framing, are not candidates for delegation. The verification pass on every artifact that enters the sales motion is not optional. The accountability is not transferable.

The discipline does not shrink when AI gets better. It becomes more important. When every competitor's field team is producing AI-generated technical content at the same volume, the differentiator is the technical marketer who can verify what that content actually claims, hold the company's honest engineering reality in the same hand, and decide what to put in front of the analyst on Tuesday. That practitioner is not being replaced by the tooling. They are being revealed by it.

08 · The New Context

Where the discipline is being built from scratch

The most significant development in technical marketing right now is not happening inside enterprise software companies. It is happening at AI companies, where the function is being built from scratch, under the hardest version of the technical marketing problem that has ever existed.

An AI company selling to enterprise buyers faces a problem no enterprise software company faced before 2023. The product is the most technically complex thing the buyer has ever been asked to evaluate. The claims (model accuracy, context window behavior, reasoning reliability, agentic task completion, safety properties in regulated environments) are claims that fail in verifiable, expensive, and embarrassing ways when they are overstated. The buyer has their own AI engineers. Those engineers will test the claims. The tolerance for almost right is the same as it has always been in a technical sale: zero.

OpenAI building an enterprise GTM motion. Anthropic selling to regulated industries. Cohere positioning against the foundation models. Mistral establishing technical credibility in Europe. Scale AI justifying data and evaluation services to buyers who are themselves building AI systems. Every one of these companies needs a practitioner who can hold the honest technical reality of their AI system in one hand and the buyer's evaluation question in the other, and decide what to put in front of the analyst on Tuesday. That is the technical marketing function. It does not yet have a name at most of these companies. The work is being done anyway, under titles that do not reflect it yet.

The demo archetype for AI companies sits between Reference Architecture and Interactive Proof on the spectrum established in Article I. The buyer's engineers want to run the model against their own data. They want to see the benchmark reproduced in their environment. They want the architecture diagram of how the model integrates with their security and compliance stack. The artifact is not a configured tenant. It is a reference implementation, a benchmark report, and an interactive evaluation environment. The Technical Marketer at an AI company builds all three and defends the claims they make.

The claims are harder to defend.
AI product claims: accuracy benchmarks, context window behavior, agentic reliability. These are testable in ways that a CRM demo or a security platform narrative is not. The buyer can run the model against their own data on Tuesday afternoon and have a result by Wednesday. The technical marketer at an AI company needs to know exactly what the model will do and will not do, and stake the company's positioning on that honest read. Overpromising fails faster here than anywhere else in enterprise software.
The buyers are more capable.
An enterprise AI buyer has AI engineers. Those engineers have opinions about context windows, fine-tuning approaches, RAG architecture, and evaluation methodology. They will challenge claims at the architectural level. The technical marketer at an AI company is not briefing a CIO who takes the demo on faith. They are briefing a principal ML engineer who has already run their own evals. The judgment required to hold that room is the same judgment that has always defined the role. The technical context is new.
The trust gap runs both ways.
AI companies face an additional layer of the trust problem: the buyer's skepticism about AI-generated content applies to the AI company's own marketing. The sales deck that a prospect suspects was generated by the vendor's own model is the deck that gets discounted at the moment of decision. Technical marketing at an AI company requires a practitioner who can produce evidence that stands up precisely because it is not what the model would have said. The human editorial layer is the credibility layer.
The function exists without the name.
At most AI companies, the work of technical marketing is being done by people whose titles are Solutions Architect, AI Engineer, or Technical Advisor. The function is recognizable to any practitioner who has done it: holding technical depth and commercial orientation simultaneously, in rooms where the next sentence determines whether the evaluation continues. The name will follow the documentation. This publication is part of that documentation.

The name will follow the documentation. The work is already happening.

09 · The Position

The structural argument, stated plainly

AI compresses the production of structured technical content across every GTM function. Sales engineering, product marketing, technical writing, developer advocacy: all of them are producing artifacts more efficiently than they were eighteen months ago. The mechanical labor of assembly is cheaper everywhere.

What does not get cheaper is the judgment that decides which artifact to produce, which claim to anchor it to, and whether the company can defend that claim in the room where the buyer decides. That judgment is the definition of technical marketing. It has always been the definition of technical marketing. The production work that surrounded it was necessary to execute the judgment. It was never the judgment itself.

The compression removes the surrounding work. What remains is the discipline's core. The discipline does not contract under AI compression. It clarifies. And what it clarifies is what it always was: editorial authority over technical evidence, applied one decision at a time, under buyer pressure, where the call matters.

The discipline does not contract under AI compression. It clarifies.

Editor's Note

The compression this article describes is not a forecast. It is the current operating condition for every senior practitioner in the GTM functions named here. The argument will sharpen as the data does.

Updated July 2026 to address Boris Cherny's prediction that the title "software engineer" may dissolve into generalist "builder" roles. Section 06 treats it as the strongest available counterexample and tests it against this article's own framework rather than setting it aside.

Practitioners who disagree with the structural position, or who have evidence that contradicts it, are invited to submit a response. The publication exists to document the discipline as it is practiced, not as it was predicted to develop.

Frequently asked questions

Does AI replace technical marketing?

No. AI compresses artifact production: drafting decks, summarizing competitive landscapes, generating deployment guides. What it cannot compress is editorial judgment under pressure. Which claim to lead with in an analyst briefing. Which architectural truth to hold when the buyer challenges it. Whether the demo shows the rough edges or the happy path. That judgment is the definition of technical marketing. It has always been the definition.

How does AI change the technical marketing role?

AI accelerates production of structured technical content, freeing the technical marketer to concentrate on the judgment work that always mattered most. The role does not shrink. It clarifies. Production work that previously consumed a substantial portion of the role's time gets absorbed by tooling. The editorial authority over which artifact to build, and which claim to anchor it to, becomes the explicit center of the discipline.

Why do technically sophisticated buyers still need human technical marketers?

Because they do not trust AI-generated technical claims at face value. Stack Overflow's 2025 Developer Survey found that trust in AI accuracy fell from 40% to 29% in a single year, with 46% of developers actively distrusting AI output. When the buyer is a principal engineer or a CISO and the decision is a seven-figure contract, the AI-generated demo and the AI-generated architecture diagram require a practitioner who can defend them under questioning. That practitioner is the technical marketer.

What is the trust gap in AI-generated technical content?

The trust gap is the distance between adoption and confidence. Stack Overflow's 2025 survey found 84% of developers using AI tools while only 29% trust the accuracy of what those tools produce. McKinsey's 2026 AI Trust Maturity Survey found 74% of organizations identify inaccuracy as a highly relevant AI risk. The gap creates structural demand for practitioners who can verify, defend, and be accountable for technical claims.

Do AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic need technical marketers?

Yes, and they are building the function from scratch. AI companies face the hardest version of the technical marketing problem: their products are the most technically complex in the current market, their buyers are the most technically sophisticated, and the claims being evaluated fail in verifiable ways when overstated. OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere, Mistral, and Scale AI all need practitioners who can defend technical claims about AI systems to enterprise buyers who have their own AI engineers. That is the technical marketing function.

What does technical marketing look like at an AI company?

At an AI company, technical marketing owns the evidence layer for AI product claims: accuracy benchmarks, context window behavior demonstrations, agentic task completion proofs, safety and compliance positioning for regulated industries. The artifacts differ from enterprise software (reference implementations rather than configured tenants, benchmark reports rather than standard demos) but the judgment work is identical. Which claim to lead with. Which architectural truth to defend. Whether the positioning holds under questioning from a buyer's technical team. The Glossary defines terms used across the discipline.

Does Boris Cherny's prediction about engineering titles apply to technical marketing?

Partly, and the precise line matters. Cherny predicts the title "software engineer" dissolves into generalist "builder" roles as AI absorbs the mechanical work of coding, which mirrors this article's own compression argument. But the real dividing line his framework exposes is not engineering versus technical marketing. It is production work, which has a correctness standard a test suite can check, versus attestation work, which has a correctness standard only a named human can stand behind. Engineering has its own attestation roles (an SRE signing a postmortem, a security engineer signing a compliance attestation) that Cherny's five archetypes don't cover either. Technical marketing is not a separate category from engineering on this axis. It is a discipline that happens to be almost entirely attestation work, which is why it reads as unusually resistant to this pressure rather than exempt from it.
