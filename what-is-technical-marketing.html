What Technical Marketing Is.

The foundational reference page. Where the discipline sits, what it produces, how it is measured, and why it is structurally positioned to do well in the AI transition.

Edited by technicalmarketing.org · May 2026
01 · Definition

A working definition

Technical marketing is the discipline that emerged when enterprise software products outpaced the language used to sell them. Its practitioners hold technical depth and commercial orientation simultaneously and translate between the two for every audience that needs to evaluate the product.

The role exists at every major enterprise software company. Its title varies. Technical Marketing Manager. Technical Product Marketing Manager. Principal Technical Marketing Engineer. Director of Technical Marketing. The work, where it is done well, is recognizably similar across companies even when the titles are not.

Red Hat's published description of the role frames the technical marketer as someone who creates technical content, supports developer relations elements, and executes campaigns aimed at technical audiences. That definition is accurate as far as it goes. It captures the visible output of the role rather than its underlying function.

The work itself is more specific. Technical marketing builds the evidence and story layer that supports a complex technical sale. The output takes many forms. The function is constant: produce the artifacts that allow a technically capable buyer to evaluate the product on its own terms, and the commercial structure that allows a sales team to use those artifacts to close.

02 · Function Map

Where the discipline sits

The clearest way to understand technical marketing is to plot it against two dimensions: technical depth and commercial orientation. Most disciplines that touch enterprise software products score high on one axis and low on the other. Technical marketing requires both.

Figure 01. The function matrix. Technical marketing is the only discipline that requires high scores on both axes. The combination is rare, which is why the role is hard to staff and hard to define.

Engineering operates at high technical depth and low commercial orientation. Its work is true regardless of how the product is sold. Brand operates at low technical depth and high commercial orientation. Its work shapes the company's identity rather than the product's mechanism. Product marketing operates at moderate technical depth and high commercial orientation. It owns positioning, pricing, launch, and the customer-facing narrative.

Sales engineering operates at high technical depth and moderate commercial orientation. It applies technical knowledge in service of specific deals. Technical marketing is the discipline that requires high scores on both axes. It needs enough technical depth to engage credibly with engineering and to evaluate product claims. It needs enough commercial orientation to know which evidence is persuasive at which stage of a buying process.

The combination is rare. Most practitioners arrive at the role from one side and learn the other. The companies that win the most technical evaluations are usually the ones that have invested in developing practitioners who hold both natively.

03 · Distinctions

What it is not

The most useful definitions come from sharp distinctions. Technical marketing is most often confused with four adjacent disciplines.

Technical marketing is not product marketing. Product Marketing Alliance, the established community for product marketers, defines product marketing as the function that owns go-to-market: positioning, messaging, personas, pricing, and launch. That is a coherent function with a clear remit. Technical marketing is oriented differently. It is an evaluation function, not a go-to-market function. Where product marketing asks how the company should tell the market about the product, technical marketing asks what the product needs to prove, to whom, and in what form, in order to win the technical evaluation.

Technical marketing is not sales engineering. Sales engineers operate in the live technical conversation with a specific prospect. Discovery calls, proofs of concept, technical deep dives, customization conversations. Technical marketers create the structure that makes those conversations repeatable across prospects. The demo design, the evaluation framework, the competitive response playbook, the architecture overview that gets used in every enterprise account. Sales engineers execute in the field. Technical marketers architect the conditions for that execution.

Technical marketing is not developer relations. Developer relations is a community function. Its work is engagement, advocacy, ecosystem development, and the long arc of building developer trust over time. Technical marketing is a sales support function. Its work is helping technically capable buyers make evaluation decisions in finite timeframes. The skills overlap. The orientations do not.

Technical marketing is not technical writing. Technical writing produces documentation: accurate, complete, neutral. Technical marketing produces persuasion: accurate, selective, and oriented toward a specific evaluation moment. A good technical writer and a good technical marketer share a baseline of technical fluency. What they do with that fluency is different in kind.

04 · The Calibration Problem

Holding the band

Every piece of technical marketing content sits on a spectrum between two failure modes. The job of the discipline is to find the band between those modes and hold it deliberately.

Figure 02. The calibration band. To one side, the polished commercial that proves nothing. To the other, the visual whitepaper that decides nothing. Technical marketing operates in the narrow band where buyers see enough technical depth to trust the practitioner, then hand off to sales engineering for the customer-specific work that closes the evaluation.

Too much polish and the work becomes a brand exercise. The clearest example is the polished commercial: high production value, cinematic music, the product appearing in beautiful but generic shots, intended to make the viewer feel something about the company. Technical buyers watch it and disengage because there is nothing to evaluate. The content moves emotion but offers no surface against which a buying committee can test a claim.

Too much depth and the work becomes an enablement exercise. The clearest example is the visual whitepaper: an architecture diagram walkthrough, every component labeled, every flow annotated, presented as if the buyer were a new hire being onboarded to the product. Buyers learn a lot and have no idea what to do next. The commercial thread is gone. The piece teaches without persuading.

Technical marketing operates in the narrow band where a buyer can see enough depth to trust the practitioner, but not so much that they are doing engineering homework.

Technical marketing operates in the narrow band between those failure modes. A buyer watching good technical marketing content sees that the practitioner understands the product at a credible level. The depth is real. The architecture references land. The trade-offs get acknowledged rather than glossed. But the depth is deployed in service of building trust, not in service of exhaustive documentation. The buyer leaves the content with confidence that the practitioner is a competent guide, and with a clearer view of the specific question they need to bring into the next conversation.

The handoff matters as much as the band itself. Technical marketing rarely closes the decision on its own. The trust layer that good technical marketing builds is what allows sales engineers and solution consultants to step in and carry the evaluation the rest of the way. SEs and SCs do the customer-specific work: live demos against the buyer's actual data, configuration walkthroughs against the buyer's actual environment, proof-of-concept design against the buyer's actual decision criteria. Technical marketing creates the conditions. Sales engineering executes inside them. The decision happens at the intersection of both.

Holding the band is harder than it sounds because the pressure to fail in both directions is constant. Marketing leadership pushes for more polish. Engineering and product push for more depth. Sales pushes for shorter content. Customers push for deeper content. The technical marketer's central skill is holding the line against all of those pressures simultaneously, in service of the buyer's actual evaluation question.

Peter Cohan's Great Demo! methodology remains the most rigorously developed framework for one piece of this work. The Demo Coherence Index is a more recent attempt to score the broader question of whether demo content actually proves what it claims to prove across multiple formats. Both are responses to the same underlying problem: the calibration question has never had a shared standard, and the field has been operating without one.

05 · Evaluation Lifecycle

Where the work concentrates

The other useful frame for understanding technical marketing is the buyer's evaluation journey. Most marketing functions concentrate at the top of the funnel. Technical marketing concentrates further down.

Figure 03. The evaluation lifecycle. Technical marketing reaches across the journey, contributing to product launches, analyst demos, competitive work, and customer references. The density concentrates at the evaluation phase, where qualified buyers actively compare options and test claims. The work is judged by what happens in the buying committee and in the analyst report.

Technical marketing's work is most dense in the evaluation phase, where a qualified buyer is actively comparing options and testing claims. This is where the discipline's signature work happens. Demos in every format: live walkthroughs, recorded captures, capability deep dives, format-appropriate variants for different stakeholder groups. Proof of concept scoping, where the technical marketer defines what a POC should test, what success criteria look like, and what evidence the buyer needs to take to a buying committee. Technical questionnaires and security reviews. Architecture overviews that explain the system at the level a buying committee can interrogate.

Competitive work runs alongside this. Battlecards, competitive teardowns, displacement playbooks, and the live competitive demos that show the product against named alternatives. At companies operating in crowded categories, competitive intelligence is often the single highest-leverage piece of work the technical marketing function produces.

The lifecycle frame also makes clear that technical marketing reaches further across the journey than the peak suggests. At the consideration stage, the discipline partners with Product Management on launch deliverables, building the launch demo, the technical narrative, the feature positioning, and the first-look content that establishes how the market should understand a new release. At the same stage, technical marketing produces the analyst-facing demos and briefing materials that drive the Gartner, Forrester, and IDC evaluations. Analyst demos are often higher-stakes than customer demos because they shape how the entire market perceives the product for a year or more.

At the decision and post-sale stages, the discipline contributes customer reference work, case studies built around technical outcomes, and the evidence layer that supports renewal and expansion conversations. The density tapers, but the work does not disappear.

Across the whole journey, the discipline requires different skills than the broader marketing function. The work is not about reaching the largest audience. It is about being maximally useful to the smallest audience that has the highest decision authority. A piece of technical marketing content that reaches one hundred thousand people but does not move any of them through an evaluation has failed. A piece that reaches twenty people and helps ten of them close has succeeded.

This concentration is also why technical marketing is hard to measure with the metrics that work for the rest of marketing. Impressions, clicks, and form fills are weak signals here. The relevant metrics are evaluation conversion, technical win rate, time-to-decision, competitive displacement, and analyst position. The work is judged by what happens in the buying committee and in the analyst report, not by what happens in the analytics dashboard.

06 · Practitioner Reality

The discipline as practiced

There is a reason the technical marketing manager role is one of the most inconsistently defined in enterprise software. Every company interprets it differently because no canonical definition exists for what the discipline covers or what good looks like.

At some companies the role means a former sales engineer who learned to write. At others it means a former product marketer who learned to read API documentation. At a handful of companies, usually the ones that win the most technical evaluations, it means a specialist who holds both the technical depth and the commercial frame simultaneously and can move between them without losing either.

The practitioners who do this work well almost always developed the skill through exposure rather than formal training. There is no technical marketing curriculum. No widely recognized certification. No community that has codified what good looks like the way PreSales Collective has done for sales engineers or Pragmatic Institute has done for product managers. Wendy Covey at TREW Marketing has done substantial work on the adjacent question of marketing to engineers, which captures some of the same territory. But a coherent body of practice around technical marketing itself does not yet exist in a publicly available form.

Quantitative grounding for the discipline does exist, scattered across vendor research and academic studies. Gong's analysis of millions of B2B sales conversations has produced specific findings that shape what good technical marketing content should look like. Discovery is the single highest predictor of deal outcomes. The optimal pitch length is under seventy-six seconds. The ideal talk-to-listen ratio in technical conversations is roughly sixty-five to thirty-five. Demos that demonstrate fewer features tend to win more often than those that demonstrate many. Those findings have not yet been translated into a shared body of practice for the discipline as a whole.

The field is mature in the sense that the role exists everywhere. It is immature in the sense that practitioners have no shared language for describing what they do, no agreed-upon standards for what good looks like, and no recognized place to develop publicly as members of a discipline. The work happens. The discipline has not yet been named.

07 · The Inflection

A discipline at an inflection point

The technical disciplines that move enterprise software through the market are all facing the same moment. Sales engineering, product marketing, product management, customer success engineering, developer advocacy, technical writing. Every role that produces structured technical content, runs evaluations, or translates between specialist languages is being operated on by AI agents that compress the work of production. The inflection is not unique to technical marketing. It is the condition that all of these adjacent disciplines are now navigating.

What is worth naming is that technical marketing is structurally positioned to do well in this transition, for reasons specific to where the role sits.

Figure 04. Disciplines plotted against AI exposure of artifact production and editorial leverage required. The technical roles that surround the enterprise sale share the inflection. Technical marketing sits in the upper-right quadrant: a discipline where AI can compress production substantially, but the work that wins evaluations is judgment, not output volume.

Two structural advantages worth naming.

The first is the nature of the work itself. Technical marketing's core skill is editorial judgment about evidence under pressure. The decision about what proves what, to whom, in what form, with what handoff, at what stage of a deal. AI can draft a demo script in minutes. AI cannot decide whether this particular demo, for this particular buyer, in this particular deal context, should lead with architecture or with outcome. That decision-making chain holds the buyer's specific evaluation question, the company's commercial pressure, the engineering reality, and the competitive landscape simultaneously. It is the kind of work that gets harder to delegate to a model, not easier.

The second is that technical marketing has always operated as the integrator between adjacent specialist functions. Most technical marketers come from a sales engineering, product marketing, or product background. The discipline's daily work involves translating between the specialist languages of product, engineering, marketing, sales, and the analyst community. As AI compresses the artifact production inside each of those specialist functions, the integration role becomes more valuable rather than less. Someone has to orchestrate AI-assisted output across multiple disciplines into a coherent evaluation experience. That orchestration is what technical marketing has always done.

None of this is a claim that the role is unchanged. The artifact production side of technical marketing is exposed in the same ways that adjacent disciplines are exposed. The demo gets drafted faster. The competitive teardown gets first-passed by a model. The architecture overview takes one afternoon instead of one week. The hours saved on production get reinvested in the judgment work the production was previously crowding out. Practitioners who treated the production as the job will find the role compressed. Practitioners who treated the production as the means to the judgment will find the role expanded.

This page, and this publication, exist in part to make the judgment work legible. To name what the discipline is for, so it remains recognizable when the form changes. To give practitioners entering the field today, and the practitioners doing the work now, a working reference they can build on rather than invent alone.

Frequently asked questions

What does a technical marketing manager actually do?

The core job is building the evidence and story layer that supports a complex technical sale. That includes demo strategy across formats, competitive intelligence and displacement playbooks, analyst-facing demos for Gartner and Forrester briefings, proof of concept scoping, technical content like architecture overviews and integration guides, launch partnership with Product Management, and customer reference work. The scope varies by company, but the thread running through all of it is translation: taking what the product does and making it legible to the people who have to evaluate it.

How is technical marketing different from product marketing?

Product marketing is a go-to-market function. It owns positioning, messaging, personas, pricing, and launch. Technical marketing is an evaluation function. It is oriented toward the moments where a buyer has to decide: the demo, the proof of concept, the analyst briefing, the competitive displacement deal. Success looks like winning the technical evaluation, not launching on time.

How is technical marketing different from sales engineering?

Sales engineers operate in the live technical conversation: discovery, proof of concept, technical deep dive with a specific prospect. Technical marketers create the structure that makes those conversations repeatable. The demo design, the evaluation framework, the competitive response playbook. SEs execute in the field. Technical marketers architect the conditions for that execution.

Is technical marketing a product role or a marketing role?

Both, which is why it is consistently misunderstood. It requires enough product depth to understand and communicate how things actually work, and enough commercial orientation to translate that depth into evidence that moves buyers. In practice it usually sits inside marketing, but the most effective practitioners have a product or engineering background that gives them credibility on both sides.

What skills does technical marketing require?

The core combination is technical depth, narrative construction, and commercial judgment. On the technical side: enough product and architecture knowledge to evaluate claims, build realistic demos, and engage credibly with engineering. On the commercial side: enough understanding of the buying process to know what evidence is persuasive at each stage and what risk a buyer is actually trying to de-risk.

How does the role differ at a startup versus a large enterprise?

At a startup, technical marketing is usually full-stack: one person covering demo, content, competitive, and analyst work, often working directly with founders to define the product story. At a large enterprise, the role is more specialized with defined handoffs to field marketing, demand generation, and product. The depth of any single piece tends to be greater, but the breadth of any single practitioner's scope tends to be narrower.

What metrics measure technical marketing effectiveness?

The relevant metrics are evaluation conversion, technical win rate, time-to-decision, and competitive displacement. Impressions, clicks, and form fills are weaker signals because the work concentrates in the evaluation phase rather than the awareness phase. The discipline is judged by what happens in the buying committee, not by what happens in the analytics dashboard.
