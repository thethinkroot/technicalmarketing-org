Article IV · Industrial Technical Marketing.

The demo is not the evidence.

What Virtual Twins Change About Industrial Technical Marketing.

The most valuable knowledge in an industrial company is often held by the people least able to repeat it at scale. What ABB's Water World shows about scaling expert knowledge instead of just showing the product.

01 · The Problem

The knowledge that doesn't scale

The most valuable knowledge in an industrial company is often held by the people least able to repeat it at scale.

Experienced engineers know more than the technical specifications. They know what matters, what tends to go wrong, what a less experienced buyer will misunderstand, and how one part of a system affects another. Much of that knowledge is tacit: learned through years of experience rather than from a manual.

That matters more as experienced workers retire. ARC Advisory Group's research on industrial knowledge transfer says retirement and workforce churn are making it more important for companies to capture critical operational knowledge before it leaves the organisation.1

Yet it still travels largely through face-to-face conversations, supported by slides and diagrams that depend on the expert being there to interpret them. When that expert can't attend every sales meeting, train every colleague or repeat the same foundational explanation, different stakeholders leave with different interpretations, decisions slow down, and the organisation's ability to sell and transfer knowledge becomes constrained by the availability of a few key individuals.

A demo can make a system visible and understandable, but that doesn't make it evidence on its own. Evidence enters when the buyer can use what they're seeing to test the central claim against their own situation: what changes, under what conditions, and whether that change is credible.

Information Is Not Understanding Accuracy without a mental model

Industrial companies rarely suffer from a shortage of technical information. The problem is that technical materials often assume the audience already has a mental model of the wider system. A diagram can represent a product accurately while leaving its operational and commercial meaning entirely unclear.

An experienced engineer can look at a drawing and infer where the solution sits, what it interacts with, what happens before and after it, and why it all matters. Someone without that experience can't make the same connections automatically.

02 · What the Expert Adds

Knowing what the other person must understand

The expert's real value is in making what they know useful to someone else.

They know where to begin, which details matter, what can wait, and where the audience is likely to get lost. They understand how one component affects the wider process, and they can change the explanation depending on whether they are speaking to an engineer, an operations director, a commercial lead or a CFO.

They also recognise patterns that are rarely written down: which questions usually expose a misunderstanding, which operating conditions alter the answer, which apparently minor detail may become important later. Much of this is tacit knowledge, built through repeated exposure to different sites, customers and failure points rather than captured formally in a specification.

This is why two people can present the same technically accurate material and produce very different levels of understanding. One may describe the solution correctly. The other knows how to connect it to the audience's situation, concerns and decision.

Expertise isn't only knowing the answer. It's knowing what the other person must understand before the answer becomes useful.

03 · The Mechanism

Scaling the expert

Scaling the Expert Making the repeatable parts of an explanation available without requiring the expert to deliver it every time

Not reproducing the expert. A well-designed virtual environment carries the repeatable foundational explanation, built with the subject matter expert's input on what to show, in what order, and which connections matter. The expert is still needed for site-specific judgement; their time is better spent there than repeating explanations that can be made consistent and accessible.

For this article, virtual twin means an interactive 3D representation built to let people explore and understand a system. Digital twin means the more operational form: a digital representation kept connected to the physical asset or process through live or regularly synchronised data. Industry terminology isn't completely consistent, but Water World sits in the first category: its purpose is communication rather than live operational monitoring.

In many industrial organisations, the same experienced people are repeatedly asked to explain the same systems, establish the same context and correct the same misunderstandings. Their time becomes a bottleneck because the explanation still depends on their presence.

A virtual environment built only from CAD data may be visually accurate, but accuracy alone doesn't make expertise repeatable. The model represents the equipment; the experience is built around how the expert explains it.

That means a less experienced salesperson or colleague can deliver a credible foundational explanation without pretending to have the expert's level of knowledge. They can establish the context, show how the product fits into the wider process and make the central relationships visible before deeper technical questions begin.

Water World turned part of the expert's explanation into an environment others could navigate and repeat.

04 · The Proof Point

ABB Water World

ABB Water World shows what scaling the expert looks like when the idea is applied to a real industrial sales environment. ABB already had accurate product information; much of its meaning still depended on an experienced person being present to interpret it.

Water World gives a mixed audience a shared operational context. Rather than presenting ABB's products as isolated components, it places them inside a complete water treatment facility. The audience can move through the process, see where a solution sits, understand what surrounds it and follow what happens as a result of its operation.

The wet well scenario is a useful example (Figure 01). Wastewater enters the facility and must be moved onwards by pumps; ABB's variable speed drive units control how those pumps are brought online and operated. In Water World, the audience can stand inside the wet well, see the pumps in position and follow an operating scenario. When debris affects one pump, the second takes over while the first enters a cleaning sequence.

“The challenge was never simply explaining what the drive does. It was helping people understand where it sits in the wider process, what changes when it operates and why that matters.”

James Chalmers · Vice President, ABB Water

The drive units themselves are shown in Figure 02, the equipment James is describing when he talks about spending less time rebuilding the basic explanation.

James's expertise shaped what needed to be shown, which connections mattered and how the scenario should unfold. Water World doesn't contain everything he knows about water treatment. It carries the foundational explanation, allowing a salesperson to establish the process and the role of the product before James, or another specialist, steps in for the site-specific questions.

“Water World gives us a way to show the same solution in a context that makes sense to different stakeholders… that helps us get to a useful conversation more quickly.”

Bob Small · Global Marketing and Communications Manager, Water and Wastewater, ABB

05 · What This Changes

Industrial engineering communication

The wider implication is that industrial technical marketing is becoming as much a problem of industrial engineering communication as it is one of marketing. Accurate materials for technically-informed audiences only go so far when different stakeholders need to understand the same complex system well enough to make a decision.

Industrial buying decisions are made by mixed groups: engineers concerned with function and compatibility, operations leaders focused on reliability and maintenance, health and safety teams weighing risk, commercial and financial stakeholders needing to understand cost and return.

A shared operational environment gives the buying group a common frame of reference. They don't need identical explanations, but they do need to be looking at the same operational reality.

Technical marketing shouldn't remove complexity. It should help the people responsible for acting on it understand the complexity that matters.

06 · The Limits

The limits of repeatability

Immersion on its own is no guarantee that expertise will scale. If the experience is built around visual impact rather than explanation, it can fail the same way an overloaded presentation does. It can also create a false sense of completeness, if a standard scenario is clear and consistent without being right for every site or application.

The technology preserves the route through the explanation. Where that route should lead in every situation is still a judgement call.

07 · The Method

Design backwards from the decision

The most useful place to start is with the decision the buyer is being asked to make: who's influencing it, what each person needs to understand, and what claim the organisation is asking them to believe.

One useful way to test the design is to separate three levels:

Representation
Can the audience see the solution and where it sits?
Explanation
Can they understand how it works and why the wider system around it matters?
Evaluation
Can they judge whether the central claim is credible in their own situation?

Begin with the decision. A successful virtual environment is the representation that best helps the audience understand and evaluate it.

08 · The Test

End with the test

The quality of an industrial demonstration shouldn't be judged by how much it shows, how realistic it looks or how impressed the audience appears while using it. The better test is what remains once the expert has stopped speaking.

A successful demonstration doesn't require every stakeholder to leave with the same level of technical knowledge. It should leave them with enough shared understanding to evaluate the central claim from their own perspective.

After the demonstration, can the different stakeholders explain not only what the product is, but why its central claim is credible in their context?

Frequently asked questions

What is industrial technical marketing?

Industrial technical marketing is the practice of helping technically capable buyers understand and evaluate complex industrial products, systems, and engineering claims in the context in which they operate. It connects technical accuracy with the operational, commercial, and decision context a buying group needs to act.

What does “scaling the expert” mean in industrial technical marketing?

It means making the repeatable parts of an expert's explanation available without requiring the expert to deliver it in person every time. It does not mean replacing the expert. Site-specific judgment, objections, and decisions made under uncertainty still require the expert; scaling removes the burden of repeating the same foundational context.

What is the difference between a digital twin and a virtual twin?

In this article, virtual twin means an interactive 3D representation built to let people explore and understand a system. Digital twin means the more operational form, a digital representation kept connected to the physical asset or process through live or regularly synchronised data. Industry terminology isn't completely consistent across companies, but ABB Water World sits in the first category: its purpose is communication rather than live operational monitoring.

How can virtual twins help industrial sales and technical marketing teams?

Virtual twins can give industrial sales and technical marketing teams a repeatable way to establish system context before deeper technical discussion begins. Their value is not simply visualizing equipment. A well-designed environment can show where a product sits, what it interacts with, what changes when it operates, and why those relationships matter to different stakeholders.

How should an industrial product demonstration be evaluated?

Evaluate it at three levels: representation, explanation, and evaluation. Can the audience see the solution and where it sits? Can they understand how it works and why the wider system around it matters? Can they judge whether the central claim is credible in their own situation? A demonstration that only achieves representation looks impressive but doesn't help a buyer decide.
