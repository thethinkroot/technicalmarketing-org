Article I.

The Technical Marketer: A Canonical Job Description.

Wanted.

The Technical Marketer.

A job description for a role most companies have not yet written. Six workstreams. Six measurable outcomes. Three compensation bands. A copyable text for use in real hiring.

Edited by Chad Corriveau, Founding Editor · May 24, 2026 · Updated June 12, 2026 · 18 min read
01 · The Premise

What the work is

The work is deciding what technical evidence the company puts in front of its hardest audiences. Which two claims to lead with in an analyst submission. Whether a demo shows the rough edges or the happy path. Which competitor weakness the field should lean on. The work is editorial judgment about evidence, made one decision at a time, under buyer pressure.

The artifacts the role produces vary. A demo for an analyst. A teardown of a competitor's launch. A first-call narrative for an enterprise rep. A reference architecture for a buying committee's principal engineer. A response to the security questionnaire that lands without overpromising. The artifacts are different. The judgment that decides which artifact to produce, in which order, with which framing, is the same judgment every time.

That judgment is the work. Most companies that depend on it have not written it down as a job description.

The artifacts vary. The judgment is the work.

02 · The Inflection

Why the moment names it

The technical disciplines that move enterprise software through the market are all facing the same compression. Sales engineering, product marketing, product management, technical writing, developer advocacy. Every function that produces structured technical content is being operated on by AI agents that compress the work of artifact production. The inflection is not unique to technical marketing. It is the condition that all of these adjacent disciplines now share.

What can be compressed is artifact production. Models can draft a deck. Models can summarize a competitive landscape. Models can generate a deployment guide. The mechanical labor of producing structured technical content is being absorbed by tooling that did not exist eighteen months ago.1

What cannot be compressed is editorial judgment under pressure. Which slide an analyst challenges. Which two claims a Tuesday call will pressure-test. Whether the architecture diagram should lead the briefing or close it. Those decisions hold the buyer's specific evaluation question, the company's commercial pressure, the engineering reality, and the competitive landscape simultaneously. They are not the kind of work that gets easier to delegate to a model.2

Figure 01. Disciplines plotted against AI exposure of artifact production and editorial leverage required. Technical marketing sits in the favorable quadrant. AI compresses production substantially across all six disciplines. The work that wins evaluations is judgment, not output volume.

This is the structural reason to write the job description down now. The discipline does not contract under AI compression. It clarifies. The artifact production that previously consumed the role's time gets absorbed by tooling. The judgment work that previously sat unrecognized in the gaps between artifacts becomes the explicit center of the role. What gets documented as a job description today is not the role's reduction. It is the role's distillation to the work that always mattered.

The discipline does not contract. It clarifies.

03 · The Role

A working definition

Definition Technical Marketer (TMM)

The editorial authority on what technical evidence a company presents to its most skeptical audiences. The role decides which claims to lead with in analyst briefings, which demo proves the positioning at which stage of a deal, and which competitive argument the field uses under pressure. It sits one degree of separation from quota.

A Technical Marketer is the editorial authority on what evidence the company presents to its most skeptical audiences. Analyst briefings. Buyer demos. Competitive engagements. Earnings-adjacent positioning. Reference customer conversations. The role is decided one judgment call at a time, in rooms where the next sentence determines whether the meeting goes forward.

The role is senior because the judgments are senior. The same call about which competitor weakness to lead with can land a contested deal or burn a customer relationship. The same call about which architectural claim to lead with in an analyst briefing can move a position in a Gartner Magic Quadrant or stall one for a cycle. The judgments compound in both directions. Senior practitioners get the calls right more often. That is the reason the role is senior.

04 · What the Role Delivers

Four outcomes the company cannot get elsewhere

Most adjacent functions touch some of this work. Product marketing owns positioning and launch. Sales engineering owns the technical conversation in active deals. Product management owns the roadmap and the build narrative. Each of those functions contributes to the technical evidence layer. None of them owns it.

A defensible technical narrative.
Someone has to hold the line on the company's technical claims when buyers pressure them. The Technical Marketer is the person whose job description includes defending the claim and revising it when the engineering reality moves.
Consistency in the field.
Every Sales Engineer and Sales Consultant runs the same validated demo, leads with the same positioning, fields the same competitive question the same way. Without the role, every SC builds their own demo and the company's technical narrative fragments across the field.
Speed from claim to evidence.
When the product makes a new claim, someone has to produce the demo, the architecture diagram, the proof point, the competitive comparison, and the answer-to-the-question that the claim will provoke. The Technical Marketer produces those artifacts faster than any adjacent function because the role is designed for it.
Competitive truth.
The role owns the company's honest read of where it stands against named competitors. Not the read the deck shows the field. The read leadership uses to decide which positioning to commit to. The two reads are often different. The role is responsible for both.

The T in Technical, the truth in marketing.

05 · Responsibilities

Six workstreams. One discipline.

The six workstreams below appear, in varying combinations and weightings, in every Senior Technical Marketing Manager job description published by major enterprise software companies in the last twelve months.3 They are the operating surface of the role.

Demo authoring and live delivery.
Building demos that work in front of analysts, in front of buying committees, and in front of camera for recorded distribution. Owning the demo's substance, its calibration, and its handoff to the field.
Analyst positioning across Gartner, Forrester, and IDC.
Producing the analyst-facing materials, briefing the analysts, defending the company's position during evaluation cycles, and translating the analyst feedback back to product and field.
Narrative architecture across launches and earnings.
Designing the technical narrative that the launch, the analyst day, and the earnings call all draw from. The architect role rather than the writer role.
Technical content for engineers, architects, and CIOs.
Reference architectures, integration guides, deep-dive briefings, customer-facing technical documentation that lives outside Product Documentation proper. The content that lets a buying committee evaluate the product on its own terms.
Sales engineering enablement and field readiness.
Producing the demo recipes, the discovery questions, the competitive responses, and the technical proof points that Sales Engineers use in live deals. Building the conditions for repeatable technical wins.
Competitive positioning under pressure.
Naming the competitor. Producing the teardown. Building the displacement playbook. Briefing the field on the comparative argument. Carrying the company's honest read of competitive position into every artifact the role produces.
06 · Outcomes

What it delivers, measured

The role is judged by what happens in the rooms it influences, not by what happens in the dashboard. The six measures below are what the company can read on the scoreboard.

Analyst position shift in the next AR cycle.
Movement in Gartner Magic Quadrant, Forrester Wave, IDC MarketScape positions. The single most public measure of the role's effectiveness.
Win rate on contested deals.
Deals where the company is in active competitive evaluation. The role's enablement of the field shows up directly in this number.
Field activation across SCs and AEs.
Percentage of the field running the validated demo, using the current battlecards, leading with the validated positioning. Lagging activation signals the role's content is not landing or is too far from the field's reality.
Time from claim to defensible evidence.
When the product team commits to a new claim, how long does the field have to wait for the demo, the proof point, the architecture diagram, and the competitive teardown that defends it. The shorter this time, the more strategic the role.
Competitive displacement in named accounts.
Number of deals won that came from a named competitor's installed base. A direct read of the role's competitive intelligence and field enablement.
Pipeline contribution through field activation.
Pipeline attributed to the technical content the role produces. Not the same as marketing-sourced pipeline. This is pipeline that field reps and SCs source because the technical content gave them something to lead with.

Technical marketing sits one degree of separation from quota. The Sales Engineer's win is the role's win. The analyst's report is the role's report. The role is judged by results the field and the analysts produce, not by results the role produces directly.

07 · Where the Role Sits

Who the role works with

The role's organizational position varies by company stage. The collaboration surface does not. The same set of adjacent functions touches the role's work at every company that runs an enterprise software motion. The exclusion is also the same. The role does not work with engineering proper, because the work happens downstream of build, on the evidence and narrative layer rather than on the product itself.

Figure 02. The function matrix. Technical marketing is the only discipline that requires high scores on both technical depth and commercial orientation. The combination is rare, which is why the role is hard to staff and hard to define.

Reports to

The reporting line varies by company stage. The variants are addressed in detail in Section 08.4

Works with daily

Product Management. Product Marketing. Sales Engineering. Analyst Relations. Sales Enablement. Customer Success. Customer Marketing. The role does not lead any of these functions. It produces the technical evidence and editorial direction that each of them needs.

Does not work with

Engineering proper. The work happens downstream of build. The role consumes engineering's output and translates it into evidence the field and the market can evaluate. The role does not influence what gets built. It influences how what gets built is understood.

08 · Variant Adaptations

How the role looks at each company stage

The role's responsibilities and outcomes are stable across companies. The role's organizational position, scope of authority, and daily counterparts vary substantially. The three most common variants are documented below.

Platform companies

Public or late-stage platform companies running a full enterprise motion (ServiceNow, Salesforce, Workday, Atlassian). The role reports to a Director or VP of Technical and Product Marketing inside the CMO organization. Title is usually Senior Technical Marketing Manager or Principal Technical Marketing Engineer. The scope is deep, vertical, and analyst-heavy. The role owns one product area (ITSM, CRM, HCM) or one buyer audience (CIO, CISO, CDO) and partners with a counterpart Product Marketing Manager who owns positioning and launch. Day-to-day, the role works most closely with Sales Engineering and Analyst Relations. Most of the visible output is analyst-facing demos, reference architectures, and competitive teardowns.

Infrastructure and data companies

Companies whose buyer is technical (CrowdStrike, Snowflake, Databricks, Confluent, MongoDB). The role often reports through Product Marketing leadership inside the CPO organization rather than under the CMO. This is because the product complexity demands a closer reporting line to the product organization than a marketing organization can offer. The role spends substantial time with Product Management and Sales Engineering. Less time with Analyst Relations than at platform companies, because the analyst evaluations matter less when the buyer is already a credentialed practitioner. The visible output is heavy on technical content (reference architectures, deployment guides, integration patterns) and on enabling Sales Engineers to win technical evaluations against named competitors.

PLG companies

Product-led growth companies where the product itself is the primary sales motion (Linear, Vercel, Supabase, PostHog, Notion). The role often reports directly to the Head of Marketing rather than through a separate Technical Marketing chain, because the company is too small to have built the deeper marketing organizational structure. The role combines technical authority with growth-loop responsibility. Less analyst work, more developer-facing content. Less competitive teardown, more category-defining narrative. The role is closer to Developer Relations and Documentation than at the other two company types. Comp tends to be lower at base but higher in equity if the company is pre-public.

Figure 04. Demo archetype by company type. The artifact changes. The discipline does not. AI companies now appear across all four archetypes simultaneously — running Benchmark Artifacts to establish model credibility, Configured Environments to win enterprise deals, Reference Architectures for developer evaluation, and Interactive Proofs to drive adoption at scale.

A practitioner moving between these variants will recognize the work as the same discipline but find the daily counterparts and the relative weighting of the six workstreams quite different. Hiring managers writing job descriptions for the role should be explicit about which variant they are hiring for. The role title may be the same. The day-to-day work is not.

09 · Qualifications

What the role requires

Five requirements. The first four are negotiable in combination. The last is not.

Eight or more years in technical product roles.
Some combination of Sales Engineering, Product Management, Product Marketing, or hands-on engineering work. The pattern recognition that makes the role's judgments good comes from time in technical product environments, not from a marketing background alone.3
Hands-on familiarity with the product surface.
The role builds demos and writes reference architectures. Both require hands on the product itself. Practitioners who outsource their familiarity to engineers cannot move at the speed the role demands.
The capacity to defend technical claims under questioning.
Analyst briefings and executive customer conversations both require holding the company's technical position while a credible technical interrogator pressures it. This skill is not the same as technical depth. Many technically deep practitioners lose composure under direct questioning. The role requires both the depth and the composure.5
Comfort in front of analysts, executives, and customers.
The role is not a back-office role. The work surfaces in front of buying committees, on customer advisory boards, in analyst briefing rooms, and on conference stages. Practitioners uncomfortable with that surface area should expect to be exhausted by the role within a year.
Editorial judgment about which evidence matters.
The non-negotiable requirement. Every other qualification supports this one. The role is not about producing the most evidence. It is about producing the right evidence, in the right form, at the right moment, for the right audience. Practitioners who confuse output volume with editorial judgment fail in the role even when they are technically excellent.2

The candidate must still answer the question after the polish comes off. Polish is table stakes. Substance under pressure is the qualification.

11 · The Job Description

A copyable text for use in real hiring

The text below is structured for direct use. Copy, adjust the company-specific paragraph, calibrate the compensation band to your variant, and post. The language has been tested for legal review at one of the named platform companies. Adjustments may be required for your jurisdiction.

Job Description · Senior Technical Marketing Manager

Senior Technical Marketing Manager

[Company Name] · [Location or Remote] · Full-time

About the role

We are looking for a Senior Technical Marketing Manager to own the technical evidence and narrative layer that supports our enterprise sales motion. You will be the editorial authority on what evidence we present to analysts, buyers, and the field. You will build the demos that our Sales Engineers run, the analyst-facing materials that move our Gartner and Forrester positions, the competitive teardowns that win contested deals, and the technical content that gives our customers a clear read on what we do and where we stand.

This is a senior individual contributor role. You will report to [Director / VP] of Technical and Product Marketing. You will work daily with Product Management, Product Marketing, Sales Engineering, Analyst Relations, and Sales Enablement. The role's success is measured by analyst position movement, win rate on contested deals, field activation, time from claim to evidence, competitive displacement, and pipeline contribution.

What you will own

  • Demo authoring and live delivery for analysts, buyers, and the field
  • Analyst positioning across Gartner, Forrester, and IDC evaluation cycles
  • Narrative architecture for product launches and earnings-adjacent positioning
  • Technical content for engineers, architects, and CIOs
  • Sales Engineering enablement and field readiness
  • Competitive positioning, displacement playbooks, and the company's honest competitive read

What you will be measured on

  • Analyst position shift in the next AR cycle
  • Win rate on contested deals
  • Field activation across Sales Consultants and Account Executives
  • Time from product claim to defensible evidence in the field
  • Competitive displacement in named accounts
  • Pipeline contribution through field activation

What we are looking for

  • Eight or more years in technical product roles: Sales Engineering, Product Management, Product Marketing, or technical content with hands-on product depth
  • Hands-on familiarity with [Company's product domain]
  • The capacity to defend technical claims under questioning from analysts, executive customers, and competitive challenge
  • Comfort in front of analysts, executives, customers, and on stage at industry events
  • Editorial judgment about which evidence matters at which stage of an evaluation

Compensation

Base $[140K to 200K] · Total compensation $[200K to 330K] depending on experience and location. Equity, full benefits, and [company-specific perks].

This is Article I of Volume 01. The publication exists because the discipline has been doing serious work for fifteen years and has not had a public reference written for it.
