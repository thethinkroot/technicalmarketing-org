Glossary of Technical Marketing.

Terms.

The vocabulary of the discipline, defined.

A working reference for the terms practitioners use. Definitions as the field applies them, not as a dictionary would.

Maintained by Chad Corriveau · Founding Editor · Technical Marketing

Every discipline accumulates vocabulary before it accumulates definitions. Technical marketing is no exception. The terms below are defined as the field uses them in practice: in job descriptions, in deal reviews, in conversations between practitioners who know what the work is and are trying to name it precisely.

Scope. This glossary covers the operational vocabulary of technical marketing as practiced across enterprise software, infrastructure, AI, and hardware. Definitions prioritize field usage over etymological correctness. Where a term is used differently across companies or contexts, the definition reflects the most common practitioner interpretation.

For the foundational definition of the discipline itself, see What Is Technical Marketing?

A

Agentic AInoun

AI systems that plan and execute multi-step tasks autonomously, using tools, APIs, and external data sources, rather than responding to a single prompt and stopping.

The distinction from generative AI is operational rather than architectural. A generative model answers a question. An agentic system pursues a goal through a sequence of actions, evaluating intermediate results and adjusting. In enterprise software, the shift to agentic deployment is the primary driver of the trust gap: buyers who accepted a model that drafted text are more cautious about a model that executes workflows, modifies records, and triggers external processes.

For technical marketing, agentic AI is both a product category to market and a production accelerator. Gartner projected that 40 percent of enterprise applications would feature task-specific AI agents by 2026. McKinsey documented that nearly two-thirds of enterprise buyers cite security and risk concerns as the top barrier to scaling agentic AI deployment. The technical marketer's job in this environment is not to argue that the concerns are wrong. It is to produce the evidence: demos, architecture diagrams, security materials that allow a technically capable buyer to evaluate the claim on its own terms.

See also Generative AI · Trust layer
Analyst briefingnoun

A structured presentation delivered to a technology research analyst at Gartner, Forrester, IDC, or a comparable firm, with the goal of accurately positioning a company's product or platform within the analyst's ongoing research and evaluation frameworks.

An analyst briefing is not a sales call. The analyst is not buying. They are forming or updating a position that will appear in research notes, Magic Quadrant evaluations, Forrester Wave reports, and advisory conversations with the analyst's clients, who are the actual buyers. A single analyst briefing can influence how an entire market segment perceives a product category for twelve to eighteen months.

Technical marketing owns the substance of analyst briefings. The demo that runs in an analyst briefing is often higher-stakes than a customer demo because the analyst is evaluating the entire category simultaneously and will compare what they see against every competitor's claim. The editorial judgment required: which architectural claim to lead with, which limitation to name before the analyst does, what level of technical depth serves the conversation, is the same judgment the discipline applies everywhere, under higher scrutiny.

Distinctions

Briefing vs. inquiry. A briefing flows from vendor to analyst. An inquiry flows from analyst to vendor. The same meeting can contain both. Technical marketing is responsible for the briefing content. The inquiry response requires equal preparation, as analysts ask hard questions and the answers become part of the research record.

See also Technical narrative · Demo
AR cyclenoun · abbrev. analyst relations cycle

The structured, recurring process by which a company engages with technology research analysts at firms including Gartner, Forrester, and IDC: briefing them on product direction, responding to research inquiries, participating in evaluation frameworks, and tracking resulting position changes in published reports.

The AR cycle is typically annual, though evaluation cadences vary by market category: major markets may see Magic Quadrant updates annually while niche categories run on 18 to 24 month cycles. The major analyst evaluation products, Gartner Magic Quadrant, Forrester Wave, and IDC MarketScape, each run on their own calendar. A company that misses a briefing window does not get another chance in that cycle. The position published in the resulting report stays on record and influences buyer decisions for twelve to eighteen months.

Technical marketing owns the substance of AR cycle participation. Briefing materials, demo content, technical proof points, and the company's honest account of where its product sits relative to competitors all flow through the technical marketing function. Analyst position shift in the next AR cycle is one of the clearest measurable outcomes the role produces.

See also Analyst briefing · Magic Quadrant · Technical narrative
Architecture overviewnoun

A structured diagram and accompanying explanation that describes how a software product is built, how its components relate to each other, and how it connects to the systems a buyer already operates.

An architecture overview is one of the most durable artifacts in the technical marketing portfolio. It serves buyers at the evaluation stage who need to understand not just what the product does but how it does it, and whether the how creates risk, complexity, or dependency. A principal engineer evaluating a platform purchase will read an architecture overview more carefully than any marketing copy the company produces.

The technical marketer's role is editorial: deciding which layers of the architecture to show, which to omit, which technical claims the diagram makes and whether those claims can be defended under direct questioning. An architecture diagram that promises simplicity the product does not deliver is a trust liability. One that accurately represents complexity, with clear explanations, is evidence of technical credibility.

Distinctions

Architecture overview vs. reference architecture. An architecture overview describes what the product is. A reference architecture describes how to deploy and configure it for a specific use case or industry. Both are technical marketing outputs. The overview lives earlier in the buyer journey; the reference architecture lives in the evaluation and implementation stages.

See also Reference environment · Technical narrative

B

Buying committeenoun

The group of stakeholders within a prospect organization who collectively influence or make a purchase decision, typically spanning technical evaluators, business sponsors, economic buyers, procurement, legal, and security functions, each with distinct criteria and distinct levels of authority over the final outcome.

No single demo, no single artifact, and no single conversation wins a buying committee. Each stakeholder role has different questions and different risk tolerances. The principal engineer is evaluating architectural fit. The CISO is evaluating security posture. The economic buyer is evaluating strategic fit and budget authority. Technical marketing builds for the entire committee, not only the technical evaluators who are most likely to attend the demo.

The structure of a buying committee shifts by deal size and company type. A mid-market deal may have three or four stakeholders with overlapping roles. An enterprise deal at a regulated company may have eight or more, including procurement and legal who never attend a product demonstration but can unilaterally stall a deal. Understanding the buying committee's composition, and which artifacts reach which members, is part of the technical marketer's strategic work on any significant opportunity.

Distinctions

Buying committee vs. champion. A champion is one member of the buying committee who has moved from evaluation to internal advocacy. The buying committee is the full set of stakeholders. A champion without coverage of the broader committee is advocating to colleagues who may not have received the evidence they need to concur.

See also Champion · Economic buyer · Technical champion
Battlecardnoun

A concise, structured reference document that equips a sales or pre-sales team member to handle a specific competitor in a live deal, covering how to position, how to respond to common objections, and what technical differentiators to emphasize.

A battlecard is a field enablement artifact. Its job is to compress the technical marketer's competitive analysis into a format a sales engineer can use in real time, in a meeting, without having to reconstruct the argument from raw research. It is a decision aid, not a research document. Length discipline is required: a battlecard that requires twenty minutes to read before a call is not a battlecard. It is a white paper with a competitive posture.

Battlecards require maintenance at a cadence the competitive landscape sets. A competitor's product launch, pricing change, or customer win can invalidate the previous version overnight. Technical marketing is responsible for the accuracy of the claims, since the field uses battlecards to make commitments to buyers, and commitments built on outdated intelligence create trust problems that outlast the deal.

Distinctions

Battlecard vs. competitive teardown. A teardown is the analytical work: a deep, structured evaluation of a competitor's product, architecture, pricing, and positioning. A battlecard is the field-ready output derived from that analysis. Technical marketing produces both; the teardown informs the battlecard, but they serve different audiences at different moments.

See also Teardown · Competitive engagement · Playbook
Briefing prepnoun

The structured preparation work a technical marketer produces before an analyst briefing, including the demo to be run, the technical proof points to be led with, the limitations to be named proactively, and the anticipated questions with prepared answers.

Briefing prep is where analyst relations either compounds or erodes a vendor’s credibility. The analyst enters with a detailed picture of the competitive landscape, recent briefings from other vendors in the category, and a set of questions designed to stress-test the claims they expect to hear. A technical marketer who has not anticipated those questions, prepared defensible answers, and rehearsed the transitions between product demonstration and technical explanation will leave the briefing having confirmed, rather than shifted, the analyst’s existing position.

The most common failure in briefing prep is optimizing for coverage rather than argument. A briefing that attempts to show everything the product does leaves no time to demonstrate anything convincingly. Technical marketing’s editorial role is to select: which claim to lead with, which proof point to anchor on, which limitation to name before the analyst does. The briefing that proactively surfaces a known weakness and explains how the roadmap addresses it builds more credibility than the one that conceals it and gets found out.

Distinctions

Briefing prep vs. briefing deck. The briefing deck is one artifact within the broader briefing prep. Briefing prep also includes the demo environment, the anticipated Q&A, the competitive context the analyst is likely to surface, and a shared understanding among everyone in the room about what the session is trying to accomplish. The deck gets sent. The prep is what makes the meeting go somewhere useful.

See also Analyst briefing · AR cycle · RFI response
Benchmark Artifactnoun

A structured technical deliverable, including performance data, comparative specifications, and reference designs, that proves a product's claim against a measurable standard. The primary demo archetype of hardware and semiconductor companies, where the audience is a procurement engineer, a standards body, or a technical architect evaluating performance under defined conditions.

A benchmark artifact is evidence-first by design. The argument is in the data: frame rates, throughput, latency, power efficiency, comparative specs against named alternatives. The practitioner's editorial work is in selecting which benchmark to run, against which baseline, under which conditions, and presenting the result with enough methodological transparency to survive scrutiny from an audience that knows how to read benchmark methodology and reject convenient test designs.

Hardware and semiconductor companies have produced benchmark artifacts formally since the 1960s. The pattern appeared later in enterprise software as performance and scalability became evaluation criteria. In both cases the benchmark artifact sits at the far right of the demo archetype axis: built for the buyer to evaluate, not for the field to run. The SE does not demo it. The buyer's engineers interrogate it.

Distinctions

Benchmark artifact vs. reference architecture. A reference architecture proves that the product can be deployed correctly for a use case. A benchmark artifact proves that the deployment performs to a stated standard. Both are evidence artifacts. The benchmark is oriented toward performance; the reference architecture is oriented toward fit.

See also Reference architecture · Configured environment · Interactive proof

C

Configured environmentnoun

A live software tenant built to prove that the platform does what the positioning claims, using realistic data, configured workflows, and the personas a buyer recognizes as their own. The primary demo archetype of platform companies, where the technical marketer owns the architecture, the data integrity, and the scenario coherence, and the sales engineer deploys it in the field.

A configured environment takes months to build correctly. It is not a sandbox and not a scratch instance. It is a managed artifact: the canonical source from which field demonstrations are derived. The data supports the demo argument. The workflows reflect an actual use case. The personas are specific enough to be recognizable and generic enough to survive across verticals. Every element is in service of a single consistent claim.

Platform companies, where the product is horizontal and the proof surface is wide, depend on configured environments more than any other company type. A ServiceNow, Salesforce, or Workday demo that runs from a poorly maintained environment exposes every integration gap, every placeholder, every workflow that was never finished. The configured environment is where the technical marketing function's infrastructure investment is most visible and most consequential.

Distinctions

Configured environment vs. reference environment. A reference environment is the canonical source. A configured environment is a specific expression of that source, built for a defined scenario, audience, or vertical. All configured environments should derive from a reference environment. A configured environment built without a reference source becomes technical debt.

See also Reference environment · Demo coherence · Benchmark artifact
Calibration bandnoun

The range of technical depth at which marketing content is most effective in a complex enterprise sale: enough depth to give a technically capable buyer a surface to evaluate, but not so much depth that the content becomes an enablement exercise rather than a persuasion instrument.

The calibration band concept describes the editorial problem at the center of technical marketing. To one side sits pure commercial content: polished, emotionally resonant, but offering no technical substance against which a buying committee can test a claim. To the other side sits pure technical depth: architecture diagrams with every component labeled, source code access, internal documentation. That content teaches everything and decides nothing.

Technical marketing occupies the band between them deliberately. The exact position within the band shifts by the audience's role in the evaluation, not their title. A technical evaluator, regardless of seniority, needs to be closer to the technical edge. An economic buyer, regardless of technical background, needs content calibrated to business impact and risk. Calibrating that position for each audience and each stage of the evaluation is the practitioner's primary editorial judgment.

Distinctions

Calibration band vs. trust layer. The trust layer is the content that occupies the calibration band. The calibration band is the concept of the right depth range. A practitioner uses the calibration band as a framework for deciding what to build; the trust layer is what results from building it correctly.

See also Trust layer · Editorial judgment · Demo coherence
Championnoun

An individual within a buying organization who actively advocates for a vendor's product internally, carries the evaluation forward when the vendor is not in the room, and is willing to stake personal credibility on the purchase recommendation.

A champion is not merely a supporter. The distinction is consequential. A supporter prefers the product. A champion sells it internally, fields objections from the security team, makes the case to the CFO, and defends the evaluation criteria against a competitor's attempt to reframe them. Deals that reach a technical win and stall before commercial close almost always lack a champion who could carry the argument past the technical evaluation stage.

The materials technical marketing produces are the tools a champion uses to do their internal selling. A champion without good materials is advocating from memory. A champion with well-constructed technical evidence is advocating from a record that survives the vendor not being in the room.

Distinctions

Champion vs. technical champion. A champion can be an economic buyer, a business sponsor, or a technical evaluator. A technical champion is specifically a technically credible individual within the buying organization who validates the product's claims to peers and to leadership. Not all champions are technical champions, but in complex enterprise software sales the strongest champions are both.

See also Technical champion · Technical win · Proof of value
Competitive engagementnoun

An active sales situation in which the buyer is simultaneously evaluating one or more named competitors, and the vendor's sales motion must account for that comparison directly.

A competitive engagement is a specific deal state, not a general market condition. The distinction matters operationally. In a non-competitive deal, the technical marketer's job is to demonstrate capability. In a competitive engagement, the job shifts: the buyer is not asking whether the product works. They are asking whether it works better than the alternative they are also evaluating. The demo, the technical narrative, and the battlecard all change character under competitive pressure.

At companies operating in crowded categories, competitive intelligence is often the single highest-leverage work the technical marketing function produces. Win rate on contested deals is one of the clearest direct measures of whether that work is landing. Technical marketing's job in a competitive engagement is not to make the competitor sound bad. It is to produce the evidence that allows a technically capable buyer to make an honest comparison and reach the right conclusion.

See also Battlecard · Teardown · Playbook
Copley Plannoun · proper term

A field-driven method for accelerating product development by embedding pre-sales and customer-success staff directly on a small, deliberately unofficial build team, rather than routing customer input through the standard product roadmap process.

Named for the Marriott Copley Place in Boston, where the approach was conceived over drinks between two colleagues frustrated with how slowly validated customer needs moved into shipped product. At MDL Information Systems in the 1990s, Peter Cohan and a colleague pulled pre-sales and customer-success staff onto a quiet, deliberately unannounced product team, compressing a roadmap item that had been sitting years out into a shipped release in a matter of months. The people who joined the team then took the finished capability back into the field and trained the rest of the organization to demo it.

The underlying principle generalizes past the original story: the people with the most accurate model of how customers actually use software are usually the ones farthest from the roadmap process, not the ones closest to it. As told in Any Questions So Far?, a conversation with Peter Cohan.

See also Field activation · Reference environment
Curse of knowledgenoun

A cognitive bias in which someone who already understands a subject finds it difficult to imagine not understanding it, causing them to skip questions or explanations that would still be valuable to the person they are speaking with.

In discovery, the curse of knowledge shows up as a practitioner skipping a question because they already know, or can guess, the likely answer. The correction is not to ask fewer redundant questions but to ask them anyway: the value of the question is rarely the information it returns to the asker. It is what the other person hears themselves say out loud, often for the first time, and the confirmation and rapport that produces. A practitioner who knows something still needs to ask it, precisely because they know it.

See also Discovery call · Reverse demo

D

Demonoun

A structured, live or recorded presentation of software in operation, designed to produce a specific belief in a specific audience at a specific stage of a sale or evaluation.

A product tour is one format a demo can take. The distinction that matters operationally is not format but intent: a navigational demo helps someone find what they are looking for. An argumentative demo is designed to produce a specific conclusion. Technical marketing builds both, but the argumentative demo is the discipline's primary concern: what to show, what to omit, what to make the audience do rather than watch are all editorial decisions made in service of a target belief.

The unit of a demo is not the feature. It is the belief the feature is meant to produce. A practitioner who cannot state the target belief before building the demo is building a tour.

Distinctions

Demo vs. proof of concept. A demo runs in a controlled environment owned by the vendor. A proof of concept runs in or against the customer's environment. The demo produces belief. The POC tests whether that belief holds in the customer's specific conditions.

Demo vs. reference environment. A reference environment is the infrastructure from which demos are produced. It is not itself a demo.

See also Demo coherence · Reference environment · Proof of concept
Demo coherencenoun

The degree to which every element of a demo: data, workflow, persona, and narrative, serves a single, consistent argument rather than demonstrating coverage of features.

A coherent demo has a thesis. The data supports the thesis. The persona running the workflow is the person the buyer recognizes as themselves. The sequence of screens follows an argumentative logic, not a product navigation logic. A demo that covers many features but argues for nothing is incoherent regardless of its technical completeness.

Demo coherence is the primary quality signal for technical marketing output. Coverage is sometimes the correct brief: an analyst evaluating platform breadth, or a horizontal product selling to multiple personas simultaneously, may require a demo that demonstrates range rather than a single argument. The discipline is knowing when coverage serves the evaluation and when it defeats it. The most common failure is defaulting to coverage when argument was the right choice.

See also Demo · Reference environment
Demo environmentnoun

A configured instance of a software product, built specifically to support sales and evaluation activities, as distinct from production, staging, or development environments.

A demo environment is purpose-built for argument. Its data is curated, not sampled. Its configuration reflects the buyer's world, not the vendor's internal defaults. Its stability is a requirement. A demo environment that breaks during a live presentation is a trust event, not a minor inconvenience.

Demo environments accumulate debt faster than any other environment type. The pressure to add new product capabilities before they are demonstrably coherent, combined with the absence of a clear owner, produces environments that cover the product map but argue for nothing. Technical marketing is responsible for the editorial integrity of the demo environment, not merely its technical maintenance.

See also Reference environment · Demo
Displacementnoun · also competitive displacement

A sales motion in which a vendor wins business from a buyer currently using a named competitor's product, requiring the vendor to overcome switching costs, existing relationships, and an incumbent advantage in addition to making the affirmative case for their own product.

Distinctions

Displacement vs. expansion. Displacement takes business from a competitor. Expansion grows business within an existing customer. Both are pipeline contribution metrics for technical marketing, but they require different content: displacement requires competitive battlecards and proof of switching value; expansion requires reference architecture and customer success evidence.

See also Playbook · Battlecard · Competitive engagement
Discovery callnoun

A structured conversation between a vendor's sales or pre-sales team and a prospect, conducted early in the sales cycle to understand the buyer's current environment, the problem they are trying to solve, the criteria they will use to evaluate solutions, and the decision-making process they will follow.

Discovery is the diagnostic stage of a complex sale. Done well, it tells the SE which demo to build, the technical marketer which proof points to prepare, and the AE which objections to anticipate. Done poorly, it produces a demo built for the wrong audience, proof points that do not address the buyer's actual concerns, and a deal that stalls because the vendor never understood what the buyer needed to be confident enough to decide.

Technical marketing's relationship to discovery is indirect but consequential. The function does not typically conduct discovery calls, but it builds the frameworks that make discovery consistent: the question guides, the technical discovery templates, the qualification criteria that tell the SE which information to gather and why. A field team running discovery without a framework is gathering information for a deal. A field team running discovery with a technical marketing framework is gathering information that makes the entire portfolio of sales materials more useful.

Distinctions

Discovery vs. technical discovery. Discovery is the broad conversation about the buyer's situation, goals, and decision process. Technical discovery is the deeper, SE-led phase focused on architecture, integrations, data flows, security requirements, and the technical constraints that will determine whether the product fits.

See also Sales engineer / Solutions consultant · Buying committee · Proof of concept

E

Editorial judgmentnoun

The practitioner's capacity to decide, under commercial pressure, which technical evidence to present to which audience, in what order, with what framing, and at what level of depth, such that the result advances a specific buyer's evaluation rather than satisfying the vendor's instinct to show everything.

Editorial judgment is the defining skill of technical marketing and the dimension most resistant to compression by AI tooling. A model can draft a competitive summary in minutes. It cannot decide whether to lead with the architectural weakness or the pricing gap, because that decision depends on which competitor the prospect is currently evaluating, what the analyst who will see this deck actually cares about, and what the engineering team will actually stand behind if pushed. Those inputs are not in the training data. They are in the room.

The concept borrows from editorial practice in publishing: an editor does not write everything, but decides what gets written, what gets cut, what leads, and what follows. The technical marketer applies the same function to the evidence layer of a complex sale. Every artifact the role produces is the result of editorial judgment about what the buying committee needs to see and what they do not.

Distinctions

Editorial judgment vs. content production. Content production is the mechanical work of creating an artifact. Editorial judgment is the decision layer above it: deciding which artifact to build, which claims to anchor it to, and whether the result will hold under buyer pressure. AI has compressed content production. It has not compressed editorial judgment.

See also Calibration band · Trust layer · Demo coherence
Evaluation conversionnoun

The rate at which opportunities entering a formal technical evaluation stage result in a technical win, expressed as a percentage and used as a primary measure of technical marketing effectiveness at the stage where the discipline's work is most concentrated.

Evaluation conversion is the most direct signal that technical marketing content is working at the stage where it matters. Impressions, clicks, and form fills measure awareness and interest. Evaluation conversion measures whether the discipline's output is actually winning evaluations. A function with high awareness metrics and low evaluation conversion is producing content that attracts interest but does not survive scrutiny.

Improving evaluation conversion requires understanding where evaluations fail. A deal that reaches a proof of concept and does not convert to a technical win is a different failure than a deal that fails in the initial demo. Technical marketing investigates both: the POC failure may indicate a product gap or a scoping problem; the demo failure may indicate a coherence or calibration problem.

See also Technical win · Time to decision · Field activation
Economic buyernoun

The individual within a buying organization who holds final budget authority and whose approval is required to commit to a purchase, regardless of the technical or business consensus that precedes their involvement.

The economic buyer is often not the person running the evaluation. In a typical enterprise software deal, the technical evaluators, business sponsors, and champions have all formed a view before the economic buyer formally engages. Their role is not to re-evaluate the product. It is to determine whether the strategic priority and financial commitment are warranted. A deal that has won the technical evaluation and the business case can still fail at the economic buyer if the value framing does not land at the level of business impact and financial risk they are accountable for.

Technical marketing's relationship to the economic buyer is indirect. The function rarely presents to them directly. But the materials it produces, the business value narrative, the competitive positioning, the proof points that survive scrutiny, are what the champion uses to make the case in meetings the vendor cannot attend. Building content that works at the economic buyer's level of abstraction, without losing the technical credibility that justified the evaluation, is one of the harder calibration problems in the discipline.

Distinctions

Economic buyer vs. technical evaluator. The technical evaluator determines whether the product can do what is claimed. The economic buyer determines whether the purchase is worth making. A product can win the technical evaluation and lose the economic buyer conversation if the business case was never translated from technical terms into financial and strategic ones.

See also Buying committee · Champion · Technical win

F

Field activationnoun

The percentage of the sales and pre-sales field team actively using the current validated demo, the current competitive battlecards, and the current technical positioning in live customer conversations, used as a measure of whether technical marketing content is reaching and being used by those it is built for.

Field activation is a lagging indicator of technical marketing effectiveness. Content that is produced but not used by the field is not a success. A set of field-facing environments that only three SCs out of thirty are using signals that something in the activation chain has failed: the content, the communication, or the fit. Measuring field activation requires tracking which materials are actually running in deals, not which materials have been published to a sales portal.

Low field activation is diagnostic. It can indicate content that does not fit how SCs are actually selling, a communication failure where the field does not know the new battlecard exists, or content that is technically accurate but not usable in the time constraints of a real customer conversation. Each failure mode has a different remedy.

See also Sales engineer / Solutions consultant · Playbook · Evaluation conversion

G

Generative AInoun

AI systems that produce new content: text, images, code, and audio, by learning statistical patterns from large training datasets and generating outputs that match a prompt's intent without retrieving stored responses.

In the context of technical marketing, generative AI operates on two planes simultaneously: as a product category to market and as a production tool that changes how the discipline's work gets done. On the production plane, generative AI compresses the artifact production layer: first-pass competitive summaries, deployment guide drafts, technical slide outlines. The hours freed are real. The editorial judgment required to turn a first-pass into a defensible artifact is not compressed by the same tooling.

On the product plane, generative AI creates a specific trust problem that technical marketing is structurally positioned to address. Enterprise buyers who are evaluating AI-enabled products face the trust gap: the distance between adoption of the capability and confidence in the output. Closing that gap is evidence work: demos that show the model's behavior under realistic conditions, architecture materials that explain what the model does with customer data, and honest representation of the failure modes the product has and how it handles them.

Distinctions

Generative vs. agentic AI. A generative model produces output in response to a prompt and stops. An agentic system uses generative models as reasoning components within a larger loop that takes actions, evaluates results, and continues. Most enterprise AI products in 2026 combine both: a generative layer for reasoning and generation, an agentic layer for execution.

See also Agentic AI · Trust layer
GTMnoun · abbrev. go-to-market

The strategy, team structure, and operational model by which a company brings a product to its target market, including how it defines and reaches buyers, how it prices and packages the product, which sales motions it runs, and which functions support the customer through evaluation, purchase, and expansion.

GTM is used in technical marketing contexts most often as shorthand for the collection of customer-facing functions a product company operates: marketing, sales, sales engineering, product marketing, and technical marketing together constitute the GTM organization in most enterprise software companies. When AI is described as compressing artifact production across every GTM discipline, it means every one of these functions is producing the same type of structured content faster than before.

Technical marketing's position within the GTM organization varies by company type. At platform companies it typically sits inside marketing. At infrastructure and data companies it often sits inside product. At PLG companies it may report directly to the head of marketing. The placement affects the function's priorities but not its core work.

See also PLG company · Technical marketer · Pipeline
Gold demonoun

A comprehensive, vendor-assembled demonstration built to showcase the full breadth of a product's capabilities and differentiators, typically designed by the people with the least direct exposure to how customers actually use the product.

A gold demo is generally built to validate that a new customer-facing hire understands the product and can present it, not to fit any specific prospect's situation. Presented to a real buyer without adaptation, it reads as a harbor tour: broad, vendor-centered, and only accidentally relevant to what the prospect actually asked to see. Peter Cohan's framing, from Any Questions So Far?: a gold demo is built for the vendor, and no one else.

Distinctions

Gold demo vs. technical proof demo. A gold demo starts from the product's full capability set and shows as much of it as time allows. A technical proof demo starts from a specific prospect's stated deliverables, discovered in advance, and shows only what that prospect asked for.

See also Technical proof demo · Demo coherence

I

Industrial engineering communicationnoun

Taking complex engineering knowledge and making it understandable across the different stakeholders involved in an industrial buying decision, without losing the technical truth along the way.

Industrial buying decisions are rarely made by engineers alone. Engineers may be evaluating function and compatibility, operations leaders scalability and maintenance, health and safety teams risk, and commercial or financial stakeholders cost and return. Each is looking at the same solution but asking a different question. Industrial engineering communication is the discipline of giving that mixed group a shared operational frame of reference, engineering can't be simplified until it loses credibility, but neither can an organization assume every stakeholder will infer the wider meaning from a technical drawing on their own.

See also Virtual twin · Buying committee
Interactive proofnoun

A trial environment or sandbox that a buyer runs themselves, converting skepticism through direct experience rather than through a vendor-led demonstration. The primary demo archetype of PLG companies, where the technical marketer builds both the environment and the narrative layer that makes the experience legible.

An interactive proof inverts the demo relationship. In a configured environment, the vendor controls what the buyer sees. In an interactive proof, the buyer controls what they do. The practitioner's job is not to script the experience but to architect it: the default data, the onboarding path, the moments where the product's value claim becomes self-evident. The buyer who reaches that moment without being escorted has been more thoroughly convinced than one who watched a representative reach it for them.

PLG companies, where product adoption precedes or replaces the traditional sales motion, use interactive proofs as the primary technical marketing output. The trial environment is the demo. Field activation looks different here: technical marketing measures whether buyers who enter the trial reach the proof moment, not whether the SE ran a good session. Designing the path to that moment is the practitioner's core work.

Distinctions

Interactive proof vs. proof of concept. A proof of concept is a structured evaluation run with vendor involvement against the customer's specific environment. An interactive proof is self-directed, vendor-designed, and typically runs in a controlled sandbox. The POC is deal-specific. The interactive proof is built once and used at scale.

See also Proof of concept · Reference environment · Configured environment
Integration guidenoun

A structured technical document that explains how to connect a vendor's product to a buyer's existing systems, covering authentication, data flow, configuration requirements, and common failure modes, written at the depth required for a technical evaluator to assess integration feasibility during a purchase evaluation.

Integration guides sit at the boundary between technical marketing and technical documentation. Documentation is written for users who have already bought. An integration guide written for evaluation purposes is different in intent: it is written for a technically capable buyer asking whether integration is feasible before committing to a purchase. That distinction changes the framing, the assumed context, and what counts as sufficient depth.

A well-constructed integration guide reduces a common evaluation blocker: the buyer's security or architecture team requiring a lengthy technical review before the purchase can proceed. Technical marketing produces this artifact; it is not a documentation deliverable.

See also Architecture overview · Proof of concept · Technical narrative

L

Lead churnnoun

The loss of a prospect's future interest caused by disqualifying or under-serving them before they enter the formal sales funnel, typically through rigid qualification criteria applied to a genuinely curious but early-stage buyer.

Coined by Peter Cohan to describe what happens when a BANT-style qualification script (budget, authority, need, timeline) is applied uniformly regardless of where a prospect actually sits on the buying curve. A buyer who is "just browsing" rather than actively evaluating gets filtered out before ever seeing a demo, and does not return when the same buyer later enters an active buying process elsewhere, because the vendor's first impression was a closed door rather than an open one. The lead does not churn out of the funnel. It churns before ever entering it, which makes it invisible to funnel metrics that only measure what already got in.

M

Magic Quadrantnoun · trademark Gartner

Gartner's primary market evaluation framework, in which vendors within a defined technology category are positioned on a two-axis grid of Ability to Execute and Completeness of Vision, placing them in one of four quadrants: Leaders, Challengers, Visionaries, or Niche Players.

A Magic Quadrant position is one of the most consequential outputs of an AR cycle for an enterprise software company. Buyers actively use Magic Quadrant reports when constructing shortlists: a vendor not appearing in the relevant Magic Quadrant is often not included in an enterprise evaluation. A vendor positioned in the Leaders quadrant carries that positioning into sales conversations for the twelve to eighteen months the report is current.

Technical marketing's contribution to Magic Quadrant position is direct. The briefing materials, demo content, and technical proof points the function produces are what Gartner analysts evaluate when assessing Ability to Execute. Analyst position shift in the next AR cycle, specifically movement within or between quadrants, is a primary measurable outcome of the technical marketing function.

Distinctions

Magic Quadrant vs. Forrester Wave. Both are analyst evaluation frameworks that position vendors within a category, but they use different methodologies, different scoring criteria, and different visual formats. Both carry comparable influence with enterprise buyers; technical marketing prepares separately for each firm's evaluation process.

Mount Stupidnoun · idiom

The colloquial name for the peak of the Dunning-Kruger curve: the point at which a person with limited experience in a domain holds the highest confidence in their own competence, before further learning reveals how much remains unknown.

Practitioners at risk of standing on Mount Stupid tend to share a tell: years of tenure cited as evidence that there is little left to learn, rather than as a reason to keep asking what they might be missing. Peter Cohan's test, from Any Questions So Far?, is a single question aimed at anyone who sounds fully settled in their own expertise: is there anything you don't know that you could learn? Most answer yes reflexively. Fewer act as though they mean it.

P

Presalesnoun

The organizational function, and the set of activities, that supports a complex sale from initial prospect engagement through contract signature: including technical discovery, solution design, product demonstration, proof of concept coordination, RFP response, and security questionnaire management.

Presales is the parent category that contains the sales engineering and solutions consulting functions. Technical marketing is adjacent to presales but distinct from it. Presales operates in individual, deal-specific conversations. Technical marketing operates at the level of the product's overall positioning and builds the materials presales uses across all deals. The simplest distinction: presales runs the play; technical marketing writes the playbook.

The boundary between technical marketing and presales is one of the least standardized in enterprise software. At some companies technical marketing sits inside a presales organization and reports to a VP of Presales or Solutions Engineering. At others it sits inside marketing and is organizationally separate from presales entirely. The reporting line affects the function's incentives and priorities, but not the nature of the work: producing the technical evidence layer that makes presales conversations repeatable and winnable.

Distinctions

Presales vs. technical marketing. Presales executes in individual deals. Technical marketing builds the systems, artifacts, and frameworks that make those executions consistent and scalable. An SE who builds their own demo from scratch for every deal is doing presales without technical marketing support. An SE running a validated demo from a canonical reference environment is doing presales supported by technical marketing infrastructure.

Pipelinenoun

The aggregate of active sales opportunities a company is pursuing in a given period, typically measured in dollar value at each stage of the sales process.

Technical marketing sits one degree of separation from quota. Its work does not close pipeline directly; that is the sales function, but it creates the conditions under which pipeline can be closed. The relevant metric is pipeline contribution: the pipeline attributed to technical content, demo assets, and competitive enablement the role produces. A technical marketing function that cannot trace its work to pipeline influence is operating without accountability.

Pipeline is also used as a shorthand for the production process within technical marketing itself: the sequence from raw product knowledge to formatted, field-ready artifact. AI tooling has collapsed this production pipeline significantly. A competitive summary that required four hours now has a first-pass in forty minutes. The editorial pipeline, deciding which claims are defensible, which framings will hold under buyer pressure, has not compressed at the same rate.

See also Technical win · Playbook
Playbooknoun

A structured, repeatable guide that documents how to execute a specific type of sales motion or competitive situation, covering discovery questions, demo sequence, objection responses, and proof points, so that different field team members can run the same play consistently.

A playbook is the difference between a sales motion that depends on individual talent and one that scales with the team. Technical marketing authors the technical substance of playbooks: which demo to run for which audience, which competitive claims to lead with, which technical proof points survive buyer pressure at each stage. Sales leadership owns the commercial motion. Technical marketing owns the evidence layer beneath it.

Displacement playbooks, structured guides for winning deals against a specific named competitor's installed base, are among the highest-leverage artifacts technical marketing produces. A well-constructed displacement playbook compounds over time: each won displacement deal generates a customer reference that feeds the next cycle.

Distinctions

Playbook vs. battlecard. A battlecard is a reference artifact, consulted in the moment. A playbook is a process artifact, executed over the arc of a deal. The battlecard lives in a tab during a meeting; the playbook shapes how the deal is run from discovery through close.

Proof pointnoun

A specific, verifiable piece of evidence that supports a product claim at the level of precision required to withstand questioning from a technically capable buyer: a benchmark result, a customer outcome, a documented technical capability, or a third-party validation.

A proof point is not a claim. The distinction is the difference between "our platform is scalable" and "our platform processed 10 million events per second in an independent benchmark." The first is positioning. The second is a proof point. Technical marketing is responsible for identifying which claims require proof points, which proof points exist, and which need to be produced through benchmarks, customer references, or third-party evaluations.

Proof points decay. A benchmark from two product generations ago is not a current proof point. A customer quote from a company that has since churned is not a current proof point. Maintaining a current, accurate inventory of proof points and retiring those that can no longer be defended is an ongoing technical marketing responsibility.

Distinctions

Proof point vs. proof of concept. A proof point is an existing piece of evidence used to support a claim. A proof of concept is a live evaluation conducted to produce new evidence in a specific customer's environment. Proof points exist before the deal; a POC is created during it.

PLG companynoun · abbrev. product-led growth

An enterprise software company whose primary growth motion is driven by end-user product adoption rather than top-down enterprise sales, where the product itself is the primary acquisition, activation, and expansion mechanism.

The technical marketing function looks materially different at a PLG company than at a platform or infrastructure company with a traditional enterprise sales motion. At a PLG company, the buying decision often happens before a sales engineer is ever involved. The technical evidence layer is therefore embedded in the product experience, the documentation, and the developer-facing content, not primarily in a sales demo. The technical marketer at a PLG company is closer to developer relations and documentation than to the pre-sales function.

The role's competitive positioning work is also different in a PLG motion: less focus on named-account displacement, more focus on category narrative: defining what the product category is before a competitor can define it differently. Field activation metrics are weaker signals in a PLG context; product-qualified lead conversion and expansion within existing accounts are stronger indicators of technical marketing effectiveness.

Proof of conceptnoun · abbrev. POC

A structured technical evaluation in which the vendor configures and tests their product against a customer's specific environment, data, or use case, with the goal of producing evidence that the product will work as claimed.

A proof of concept is an evidence-production exercise, not a sales exercise. A well-run POC that produces an unfavorable result is more valuable than a poorly-run POC that produces a favorable impression, because the unfavorable result is honest and the favorable impression will not survive production deployment. A POC that surfaces a genuine product gap is also a product signal: the finding belongs in the product roadmap conversation, not only in the deal post-mortem.

Technical marketing is involved in POC strategy: defining the success criteria, the scope, the data requirements, and the evaluation framework before the POC begins. An undefined POC is not a POC. It is a free consulting engagement.

Distinctions

POC vs. proof of value. A proof of concept tests whether the product works. A proof of value tests whether the product produces the outcome the buyer is trying to achieve. A product can pass a POC and fail a POV.

See also Proof of value · Technical win
Proof of valuenoun · abbrev. POV

A structured evaluation in which the buyer tests whether a product produces a measurable improvement against a business outcome they have committed to achieving, rather than whether the product functions technically.

A proof of value reframes the evaluation question from "does it work" to "does it work for us in the way that matters." Technical marketing defines the value hypothesis before the POV begins: what outcome, measured how, over what time horizon, compared to what baseline. A POV without a stated value hypothesis is an extended trial with a subjective exit.

See also Proof of concept · Technical win

A metric, scanned directly from AI-generated call summaries, that scores a discovery call's quality by comparing how many summary sections describe the prospect's stated needs against how many describe the vendor's product capabilities.

Peter Cohan developed the ratio by noticing a pattern in Gong-generated call summaries: the noun-verb pair opening each summary section, “prospect seeks,” “prospect describes,” against “vendor presented,” “vendor explained,” told him instantly whether a section reflected real discovery or a pitch wearing a discovery call's schedule slot. Reading the pattern across a full call produces a rough but immediately legible score, without requiring a review of the raw transcript. As told in Any Questions So Far?, a conversation with Peter Cohan.

The ratio is notable less for its precision than for what it demonstrates: that AI call summaries, built for a different purpose entirely, can be repurposed as a fast, structural audit of discovery discipline.

R

Reference architecturenoun

A structured technical artifact, typically code, pipelines, notebooks, or integration patterns, that proves a product's claim at the level a technically capable evaluator can interrogate directly. The primary demo archetype of infrastructure and data companies, where the buyer's engineers validate the artifact, not the sales engineer.

A reference architecture sits closer to a GitHub repository than to a slide deck. The claim is embedded in the artifact itself: if the code runs, if the pipeline connects, if the notebook produces the stated output, the claim is proven without narration. Technical marketing owns the architecture's integrity: which use case it proves, whether the configuration reflects production-realistic conditions, and whether the stated outcome actually results from the work as written.

Infrastructure and data companies, including Databricks, Elastic, HashiCorp, and CrowdStrike, produce reference architectures as primary technical marketing outputs. The audience is a principal engineer or an architect, not a business buyer. The evaluation happens at a depth of technical scrutiny that a configured environment or a slide-based demo cannot satisfy. A reference architecture that contains shortcuts, aspirational configurations, or undisclosed dependencies will be found and will damage the vendor's technical credibility in ways that are hard to recover from.

Distinctions

Reference architecture vs. architecture overview. An architecture overview describes what the product is and how it is structured. A reference architecture shows how to deploy and configure it for a specific use case, at a level of technical completeness that allows a practitioner to build from it. The overview lives earlier in the buyer journey; the reference architecture lives in the evaluation and implementation stages.

See also Architecture overview · Benchmark artifact · Configured environment
Reference environmentnoun

The canonical configuration of a software product that serves as the authoritative source from which demonstrations, technical content, and evaluation materials are produced.

A reference environment is not a demo. It is the infrastructure from which demos are produced. A reference environment without clear ownership is not a reference environment. It is a shared demo instance, and shared demo instances degrade toward incoherence at a rate proportional to the number of people with commit access.

The canonical reference environment is the practitioner's primary artifact. Every field-facing demo environment should be derived from or aligned with this canonical source. Regional, vertical, or persona-specific environments that diverge intentionally still require a documented rationale for the divergence.

Distinctions

Reference environment vs. sandbox. A sandbox is a disposable environment for experimentation. A reference environment is a managed artifact for production use. Conflating them is the most common source of demo environment debt.

Regis McKennanoun · person

A Silicon Valley marketing strategist who joined National Semiconductor in 1967 and later founded Regis McKenna Inc., becoming one of the earliest practitioners and theorists of technical marketing in the semiconductor and computing industries.

McKenna's contribution to the discipline was methodological: he developed and articulated a structured approach to marketing technical products to technically capable buyers at a time when most marketing practice was built around consumer goods. His work at National Semiconductor and his advisory roles with Intel and Apple established a template for how enterprise technology companies should think about the relationship between technical credibility and commercial persuasion.

In the history of the discipline, McKenna represents the Era I anchor: the point at which technical marketing became a recognized in-house function at product companies rather than an ad hoc responsibility distributed across engineering and sales. RFPnoun · abbrev. request for proposal

A formal procurement document issued by a buying organization that requires vendors to respond to a structured set of questions covering product capabilities, technical architecture, security posture, pricing, implementation approach, and company stability, used to compare vendors systematically during a formal evaluation process.

An RFP response is one of the highest-effort, highest-stakes artifacts in the technical marketing portfolio. Technical marketing maintains the RFP content library, the approved claims, and the proof point inventory that makes responses faster and more defensible across deals. A vendor who was not part of the pre-RFP conversation is at a structural disadvantage regardless of how well-written the response is: the evaluation criteria that end up in the RFP often reflect conversations the buying committee had with vendors they already trusted.

Technical marketing's work before the RFP arrives shapes the RFP itself. Analyst engagement, reference materials, and early technical conversations with the buying committee's evaluators establish the vocabulary and criteria the buyer uses when drafting evaluation requirements. A technical marketing function that waits for RFPs to arrive is reactive. One that influences evaluation criteria before the RFP is written has a structural advantage that no response quality can compensate for.

Distinctions

RFP vs. RFI. A request for information (RFI) is an earlier, broader document used to identify which vendors are worth inviting into a formal evaluation. An RFP is issued to a shortlist of already-qualified vendors. Technical marketing responds to both, but the RFP response is the higher-stakes document and typically involves more internal coordination across product, security, legal, and finance teams.

RFI responsenoun · abbrev. request for information response

A vendor’s structured reply to a buying organization’s request for information, submitted before a formal evaluation begins, used by the buyer to assess whether a vendor should be invited into a full RFP process.

An RFI arrives earlier in the buyer’s decision process than an RFP and is correspondingly less specific in its requirements. The buyer is running a market survey, not a formal evaluation. They want to understand which vendors exist in the category, what the headline differentiation claims are, and whether a vendor’s profile is worth the effort of a full evaluation. This makes the RFI response a different kind of document than an RFP response: it needs to be sharp enough to earn a place on the shortlist, not exhaustive enough to answer every security and architecture question.

Technical marketing maintains the content library from which RFI responses are built, including approved positioning claims, representative proof points, and customer reference summaries that can be deployed quickly across multiple simultaneous RFIs. IBM’s analyst relations team documented a pattern in which AI drafting pre-populates roughly 95 percent of RFI response content, with a human QA step before submission. The pattern reflects a structural truth: most RFI questions repeat across buyers and categories, so a well-maintained content library dramatically compresses response time without sacrificing accuracy.

Distinctions

RFI response vs. RFP response. An RFI response is shorter, earlier, and less technically specific. Its goal is to earn a place on the shortlist. An RFP response is longer, later, and more architecturally detailed. Its goal is to win the evaluation. Both require technically accurate claims. The RFP response requires more internal coordination with product, security, and legal teams.

Reverse demonoun

A discovery technique in which the prospect, rather than the vendor, shares their screen and demonstrates how they currently perform a task, using their existing tool or system.

Asking "can you show me how you're doing this today?" surfaces three distinct kinds of information in one motion: what the prospect already values in their current approach, which is worth protecting rather than dismissing; where the pain actually lives, in their own words rather than the vendor's assumptions; and, often the most commercially useful, which capabilities already exist in a competitor's product that the prospect has simply never used and does not know are there. That third category is common because most people at the front end of a workflow are executing their tasks, not exploring the menus.

Ringi (禘議)noun · Japanese business term

A Japanese organizational decision-making process in which a proposal circulates for individual approval among stakeholders before a formal meeting takes place, so that the meeting itself functions as a ratification of a decision already reached rather than a forum for reaching one.

For a vendor unfamiliar with the process, a ringi-governed meeting can read as strange or even discouraging: questions arrive indirectly, sometimes rephrased several times before their real intent becomes clear, and the room can seem quiet or undecided right up until it isn't. S

Security questionnairenoun

A structured document issued by a buying organization's security, legal, or procurement team that requires the vendor to disclose and document their security controls, data handling practices, compliance certifications, business continuity posture, and incident response procedures, typically as a condition of purchase or vendor approval.

A security questionnaire is not a product evaluation. It is a risk assessment. The buying organization is not asking whether the product is good. They are asking whether the vendor can be trusted with their data and systems. A vendor who fails a security questionnaire does not lose a deal on product merit. They lose it on organizational readiness. A response to a security questionnaire that lands without overpromising, in the words of the discipline, requires the same editorial judgment as any other technical marketing artifact: stating what is true, in the way that addresses the question being asked, without creating claims the product or organization cannot support.

Technical marketing maintains the approved technical claims about the product's architecture, data handling, and compliance posture that form the source of truth for security questionnaire responses. The SE or account team produces the response, but an uncoordinated response built on unsanctioned claims is a liability that surfaces at the worst possible moment: after the technical win, during procurement, when a single inconsistency can restart the evaluation from the beginning.

Distinctions

Security questionnaire vs. RFP. An RFP evaluates whether the vendor's product fits the buyer's requirements. A security questionnaire evaluates whether the vendor's organization can be trusted as a data processor and service provider. Both can independently block a deal. Many enterprise purchases require both, and each has a different owner on the vendor side.

Sales engineer / Solutions consultantnoun · abbrev. SE, SC

A technically trained pre-sales professional who manages the technical conversation in active deals, running live demos, answering product questions, scoping proof of concepts, and providing the technical credibility that supports a commercial sales motion.

The SE or SC is the field execution layer for the evidence and frameworks that technical marketing builds. The technical marketer architects the reference environment; the SE runs demos from it. The technical marketer authors the competitive battlecard; the SC uses it in a Tuesday call against a named competitor. The relationship is structural: technical marketing creates the repeatable materials, and the SE/SC applies them in deal-specific contexts that no repeatable framework can fully anticipate.

The distinction between the two roles matters to the technical marketer because the SE/SC's experience in the field is the fastest feedback signal on whether the marketing materials are working. Win rates, demo conversion, competitive displacement in named accounts. These metrics reflect both the quality of the SE/SC execution and the quality of the technical content they were handed. When both are measured, the signal is clean. When only one is measured, the attribution is wrong.

Distinctions

SE vs. SC naming. Sales Engineer (SE) is more common in infrastructure and platform companies. Solutions Consultant (SC) is more common in SaaS and application software. The functional distinction between the two titles is small in practice; both refer to the pre-sales technical role. Solutions Architect (SA) is pre-sales at many cloud and infrastructure companies including AWS, GCP, and Azure, where SAs own the technical conversation through the evaluation stage. At SaaS companies, SA more often implies a post-sale or implementation scope. Usage varies significantly by company and should not be assumed from title alone.

SE vs. technical marketer. The SE operates in individual, deal-specific conversations. The technical marketer operates at the level of the product's overall technical positioning and builds the materials the SE uses across all deals. Technical marketing does not replace the SE; it makes the SE's work scalable and repeatable.

Scaling the expertnoun phrase

Making the repeatable parts of an expert's explanation available without requiring the expert to deliver it in person every time, as distinct from trying to reproduce the expert.

The bottleneck in many industrial organizations isn't a shortage of expertise, it's that the same experienced people are repeatedly asked to explain the same systems, establish the same context, and correct the same misunderstandings. Their time becomes constrained not because the information is unavailable, but because the explanation still depends on their presence.

Scaling the expert doesn't remove them from the conversation. Site-specific advice, unusual applications, and decisions made under uncertainty still require experience and judgment. What gets scaled is the foundational, repeatable part, so the expert's time goes toward what actually requires them.

T

Tacit knowledge transfernoun

The process of moving practical, experience-built understanding, the kind that isn't written down in a specification or manual, from an expert to someone with less experience, so decisions and explanations don't remain dependent on one person's availability.

Tacit knowledge is not the same as technical information. An organization can have accurate specifications, diagrams, and product sheets in abundance while the meaning of that material still depends on an expert being present to interpret it: what a given detail implies, which questions expose a misunderstanding, how one part of a system affects another. That interpretive layer is what tacit knowledge transfer is trying to move.

The urgency of this problem is not constant. It rises with workforce churn and retirement, when the people holding the most tacit knowledge are also the ones closest to leaving the organization, and the explanation they've been repeating in person has never been made repeatable without them.

Teardownnoun

A structured, documented analysis of a competitor's product, architecture, pricing, positioning, and publicly available technical claims, produced by technical marketing to inform competitive strategy, battlecards, and displacement playbooks.

A teardown is research before it is a sales asset. The practitioner evaluates the competitor's product as a technical peer would: testing the demo, reading the documentation, reviewing the architecture materials, evaluating the integration story. The goal is an honest read of where the competitor is strong, where they are weak, and what claims they make that cannot survive technical scrutiny.

The competitive teardown is one of the artifacts AI tooling has most directly affected. First-pass competitive summaries that once required a full afternoon now have a working draft in under an hour. The compression is real and net positive. What AI cannot produce is the editorial judgment that decides which architectural weakness to lead with in a specific deal, against a specific competitor, with a specific buyer's concerns on the table. That judgment requires knowing the buyer's context, and that context is not in the training data.

Distinctions

Teardown vs. battlecard. A teardown is the analysis. A battlecard is the field-ready output derived from that analysis. The teardown is internal; the battlecard is the artifact the SC uses in a live deal.

Technical marketernoun

A practitioner who owns the technical evidence and narrative layer that supports a complex sale: the demos, the reference environments, the competitive materials, the analyst-facing content, and the technical content that closes the distance between product capability and buyer belief.

The technical marketer sits between engineering and field, translating product reality into market language and market reality into product signal. Its outputs are environments, frameworks, and materials that others use to run deals, influence analysts, and establish category position. For the full definition of the role, see The Technical Marketer.

Technical championnoun

A technically credible individual within a buying organization who has evaluated the vendor's product, reached a conclusion about its capability, and is willing to defend that conclusion to technical peers and to business leadership as the evaluation progresses toward a purchase decision.

The technical champion is the buyer-side counterpart to the sales engineer. While the SE carries the vendor's technical argument into the deal, the technical champion carries it internally, in conversations the vendor cannot attend. A buying committee's principal engineer who has validated the architecture, or an IT architect who has run the proof of concept, becomes a technical champion when they move from evaluation to advocacy.

Technical marketing builds for the technical champion even when the technical champion is not in the room. A well-constructed reference architecture, a clear proof of concept framework, a competitive teardown that names the incumbent and explains its limitations honestly: these are the artifacts a technical champion cites when defending the purchase recommendation to a skeptical CFO or a cautious security team.

Technical narrativenoun

The authoritative, internally consistent account of what a product does, how it does it, why that matters, and what differentiates it from alternatives, maintained by technical marketing as the source of truth from which all field-facing technical content is derived.

A technical narrative is not a pitch deck. It is the underlying structure from which pitch decks, analyst materials, demo scripts, competitive battlecards, and reference architectures are all derived. When different field team members describe the product differently to different buyers, the technical narrative has fragmented. Restoring it is a primary technical marketing responsibility.

The technical narrative has a specific failure mode at companies with fast-moving products: it is written once during a launch cycle and never updated as the product evolves. A narrative that was accurate eighteen months ago and has not been maintained becomes a liability: the field makes claims the current product does not support, and the trust gap opens between what was promised and what buyers encounter in production.

Distinctions

Technical narrative vs. messaging. Messaging is the language the marketing team uses in commercial communications: short, audience-specific, designed to resonate. The technical narrative is the deeper structure beneath messaging: accurate, detailed, defensible under technical questioning. Messaging is derived from the technical narrative, not the other way around.

Technical winnoun

The point in a sales cycle at which the technical evaluators on the buyer's side have concluded that the product is capable of doing what the vendor has claimed, and that conclusion is documented and communicated to the economic buyer.

In practice, many technical wins are handshakes that go undocumented. The standard the discipline holds is higher: a technical win is a documented state change, confirmed in writing by the technical evaluator and communicated to the economic buyer. Without that documentation, what feels like a technical win is a feeling, and feelings do not survive procurement. Technical marketing is responsible for creating the conditions that produce a real technical win and for ensuring the result is captured.

Distinctions

Technical win vs. business win. A technical win resolves the question of capability. The business win resolves the question of value, priority, and timing. Deals die between the technical win and the business win when the technical success was never translated into business language the economic buyer could act on.

Time to decisionnoun

The elapsed time from the first substantive technical evaluation activity to the buyer's decision to purchase or not purchase, used as a measure of how efficiently a vendor's technical marketing content enables a buyer to reach a confident conclusion.

Time to decision is a measure of evaluation friction. A long time to decision is not always the vendor's fault: complex enterprise purchases involve procurement processes, legal reviews, and budget cycles that technical marketing cannot accelerate. But a long time to decision caused by the buyer's technical team requiring additional evidence, additional demonstrations, or additional proof of concept activities indicates that the existing technical content did not produce the confidence required for a decision at the expected stage.

Shortening time to decision is one of the practical goals of technical marketing. A proof point inventory that anticipates the security team's questions compresses the security review. A well-scoped POC framework with defined success criteria compresses the evaluation stage. A reference architecture that answers integration questions before they are asked compresses the architecture review.

Trust layernoun

The band of technical depth in a sales interaction: demos, architecture materials, competitive positioning, proof points that give a technically capable buyer enough evidence to trust the product's claims without requiring a full engineering-level evaluation.

The trust layer concept describes what technical marketing is calibrated to produce. To one side of the band is pure commercial content: polished, emotionally resonant, but offering no surface against which a buying committee can test a claim. It feels something. It proves nothing. To the other side is pure technical depth: architecture diagrams, source code access, internal documentation. It teaches everything and decides nothing. It is an enablement exercise, not a sales support function.

Technical marketing occupies the band between them deliberately. Enough depth to trust. Not so much it becomes a training exercise. The sales engineer and solutions consultant carry the conversation the rest of the way, armed with the materials technical marketing built. The calibration is the editorial judgment at the center of the discipline.

Distinctions

Trust layer vs. trust gap. The trust layer is the evidence a vendor produces to support buyer confidence. The trust gap is the distance between a buyer's adoption of a capability and their confidence in it, most visible in enterprise AI deployments, where adoption has outrun confidence in model reliability. Technical marketing's job is to close the trust gap by building the trust layer.

Talk-to-listen rationoun

A measure of how much of a sales or discovery call is spent with the vendor talking versus the prospect talking, used as a fast proxy for whether a call functioned as discovery or as a pitch delivered under the label of discovery.

The working threshold, per Peter Cohan: the vendor at or below fifty percent of total talk time on a discovery call, with anything under forty percent read as strong. In practice, vendor talk-to-listen on discovery calls is rarely under fifty percent, and demo calls run worse. A useful secondary check on the same call recording: any single vendor turn running past three or four minutes is a discovery call losing its own premise, regardless of what the aggregate ratio says.

Technical proof demonoun

A demonstration built directly from completed discovery, structured around the specific deliverables a prospect has stated they need, that starts from the desired outcome and reveals only as much underlying capability as the prospect's interest warrants.

Where a gold demo starts from the product and works outward, a technical proof demo starts from the prospect's stated deliverable and works backward into just enough of the product to prove it. Its prerequisite is discovery thorough enough to know what that deliverable actually is; without it, a technical proof demo collapses back into a gold demo with better staging.

Distinctions

Technical proof demo vs. vision generation demo. A technical proof demo requires discovery to have already happened. A vision generation demo is built for a prospect who isn't ready for discovery yet, and is explicitly not trying to be exhaustive.

Virtual twinnoun

A navigable virtual environment built to represent a physical industrial system and, more specifically, to carry the foundational explanation an expert would otherwise have to repeat in person for every audience.

A virtual twin is not the same as a CAD-accurate 3D model. A model built purely from CAD data can be visually correct while explaining nothing: it represents the equipment, not the reasoning behind it. A virtual twin becomes useful when a subject matter expert shapes what the audience sees, in what order, and which relationships between components are made visible, so a less experienced colleague can deliver a credible foundational explanation without pretending to have the expert's judgment.

The expert is not removed from the process. Site-specific advice, unusual applications, and decisions made under uncertainty still require their presence. What the virtual twin scales is the repeatable part of the explanation, freeing the expert's time for the judgment calls that can't be made repeatable.

See also Tacit knowledge transfer · Industrial engineering communication · Buying committee
Visionary engineeringnoun

A discovery technique in which a vendor introduces a capability the prospect is not yet aware of by framing it as a biased question rather than a direct pitch, prompting the prospect to request that the capability be included in their own demonstration.

The mechanism, per Peter Cohan: instead of stating a capability directly ("our software does X"), frame it through the experience of similar customers ("others in situations like yours have found that a capability we call X helped them achieve Y — is that something that might interest you?"). A prospect who says yes has now asked to see the feature in their own demo, which changes both the framing and the prospect's later memory of how the capability got there. As described in Any Questions So Far?.

See also Vision generation demo
Vision generation demonoun

A brief demonstration, typically under ten minutes, that shows a small number of representative deliverables to a prospect who is not yet in an active buying process, intended to sustain interest without forcing a premature discovery conversation.

Built to resolve the standoff between a vendor unwilling to demo before discovery and a prospect who isn't ready for discovery yet and just wants a sense of the art of the possible. Roughly half of inbound leads are commonly estimated to be in this "just browsing" state rather than active buying; a vision generation demo is the offer that keeps them engaged instead of disqualifying them out of a future conversation. See also lead churn, the failure mode this demo type exists to prevent.

Distinctions

Vision generation demo vs. gold demo. Both are used before deep discovery, but a gold demo tries to cover the product broadly. A vision generation demo deliberately shows less, on the theory that a prospect who isn't ready to buy doesn't need to see everything — only enough to stay interested.

See also Technical proof demo · Lead churn
