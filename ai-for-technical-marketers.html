AI for Technical Marketers.

One method, applied to five deliverables you actually own. Every claim below is footnoted to a named, dated source. Where one could not be confirmed against a primary source, it was cut rather than kept with a hedge.

The Problem

Every Technical Marketer has been told to “use AI.” Almost none of that advice is scoped to what this role actually owns.

Generic AI advice does not tell you where a model produces something that looks finished and fails the moment a skeptical analyst, a skeptical buyer, or an engineer with the real API spec pushes back. This guide answers one question, five times, once for each deliverable this role owns: where does AI genuinely save real work, and where does it quietly produce something you will regret shipping?

The Method

The same four steps apply to every deliverable below. Only step two changes from section to section: the specific way each one fails.

Method. Four steps, applied once per deliverable below. Step three is the only step that never changes.

01 · In practice

Paste the actual transcript, spec, or call notes into the prompt before you ask for anything. If you don’t have the source material handy, go get it before you draft. Don’t let the model fill the gap with plausible-sounding general knowledge.

02 · In practice

Before you draft, name the specific way this deliverable fails. A fabricated analyst quote, an invented API signature, a generic battlecard bullet. Naming it before you draft is what keeps you looking for it once the output is in front of you.

03 · In practice

Read every number, name, and specific claim in the output and ask: could I defend this in front of the person it’s about? If the honest answer is no, it doesn’t ship yet.

04 · In practice

Apply the one sourced move for that deliverable last, not first. It only works once the draft already reflects your real source material. It is not a substitute for steps one through three.

Before step one: know where your source material is allowed to go. “Paste in your real source material” assumes a tool your company has actually authorized for that data. A personal, free-tier AI account is not the same thing as an enterprise-licensed one, and the difference is not academic: IBM’s own AR team found that free-tier tools can retain what you type as training data, so unreleased roadmap detail, unpublished pricing, or a not-yet-public analyst conversation can leak into a model that isn’t yours.1 If your company hasn’t said a tool is cleared for that category of information, don’t paste it in. That is a company policy question to confirm first, not a productivity tip to skip.

Scope and shelf life.

This guide covers five of the six Technical Marketing workstreams†: Analyst Positioning, Narrative Architecture, Technical Content, Sales Engineering Enablement, and Competitive Positioning. It also includes a model-selection reference and a brief compass on demo-capture tooling. A full evaluation of demo-building platforms is its own dedicated guide, coming separately. What’s below is the one distinction worth understanding before you evaluate any of them.

On durability. Named tools and product features go stale in months. Structural findings (why a failure mode occurs, not which button triggers it) are the ones worth remembering. Where a finding depends on a current feature or a number a vendor could revise, it is dated. Read the dates.

WS-02 · Analyst Positioning

Briefing prep, RFI response, and one legal wall almost nobody checks.

Named AR leaders at Asana, IBM, and Sprout Social all use the same pattern: AI drafts, a human gates it before an analyst sees it.1

Where it helps

Drafting briefing-prep documents and anticipated Q&A. IBM pre-populates roughly 95% of RFI responses this way, with human QA before submission.1

Where it fails

Anything naming a specific analyst. Generative AI is “prone to total fabrication — outright lies” and should be treated as junior-level draft work.3

The fix

A human gates everything analyst-specific before it ships. Check your research license before you check your prompt.

Lesson learned

IBM’s AR team found that free-tier consumer AI tools can retain what you type as training data. Unreleased roadmap detail can leak into a model that isn’t yours.1 Lesson: use enterprise-licensed tools for anything proprietary.

Lesson learned

Gartner’s own Research Documents Usage Policy prohibits using its published research “as input into or for the training or development of generative artificial intelligence.”2 Lesson: “have AI read the analyst’s past reports for you” is not just a capability question for licensed research. It can be a licensing violation.

The move

Draft with AI. Gate everything that references a specific analyst, number, or competitive claim through a human before it goes out. Worth noting for calibration: Forrester’s own newest public briefing-prep guidance, from five named Forrester analysts in January 2026, doesn’t mention AI at all.4 The people who run the briefings still treat this as a human-craft skill.

WS-03 · Narrative Architecture

An underbuilt craft, and three model differences that will outlast this quarter’s benchmark.

No senior positioning specialist, not Dunford, Raskin, or Lochhead, has published their own AI workflow. This is genuinely open ground, not a solved problem you are late to.5,6 The closest named example of a practitioner building a living AI workflow for this work is Emily Kramer of MKT1, who maintains strategy documents inside Claude Projects.7

Where it helps

Stress-testing a positioning one-liner you already believe: deliberately attacking your own claim to find its weak points before a buyer does. Feed it in, ask the model to find holes, name three real competitors, argue why a skeptical buyer walks away.8

Where it fails

Originating positioning from scratch. The tools built on top of Dunford’s and Raskin’s frameworks are third-party implementations, not the authors’ own endorsed method.5,6

The fix

Use AI to pressure-test a draft you already believe. Never let it originate the one-liner.

Lesson learned

The only rigorous, independent test of AI citation accuracy, run by Columbia’s Tow Center across eight tools and 1,600 queries, found an overall failure rate above 60%. Perplexity posted the lowest failure rate of that field, at roughly 37% wrong.9 Lesson: that is best-of-a-bad-field, not reliable. Verify every citation any AI search tool hands you, regardless of which one.

A note on which model to use here: see the full model-selection reference further down this page for a detailed, sourced breakdown of Claude, Copilot, Gemini, and Grok. For narrative work specifically, Perplexity is the one worth a caution: see the lesson above before you trust any citation it hands you.

The move

“If it can’t argue against you, your positioning isn’t sharp enough.”8 Run that test before you ship, not after a buyer runs it for you.

WS-04 · Technical Content

Grounding beats general knowledge, every time it has been tested.

Twilio open-sourced its internal documentation prompt library in December 2025: role-separated prompts, constrained to the actual style guide, with human review still enforcing accuracy.10

Where it helps

Drafting and restructuring documentation against a locked style guide, with a distinct AI editing pass separate from the drafting pass.10

Where it fails

Letting a model recall “how APIs like this usually work.” It routinely invents method signatures that compile but fail at runtime.12

The fix

Ground every generation in your actual spec, SDK, and product version. Never general knowledge. Then verify it anyway.

Lesson learned

Plain retrieval over a docs corpus produces “hallucinated steps, mixed product versions, feature explanations that never existed,” because “similar text is not the same thing as relevant knowledge.”11 Lesson: structure your source of truth before you automate against it. A flat pile of docs is not enough.

The move

If a demo, integration guide, or reference architecture makes a claim about how an API behaves, check that claim against the real spec or SDK. Never against what a model recalls about APIs that look like it.

WS-05 · Sales Engineering Enablement

A different problem than demo scoring, confirmed by the absence of overlap.

Nothing found in this research treats demo-effectiveness scoring as an established term. The nearest concept, Consensus’s demo analytics, tracks engagement and completion.13 That is a different axis than whether a finished demo is narratively coherent.

Where it helps

Generating a wide first draft of discovery questions or demo-recipe variants, fast, for volume.

Where it fails

Deciding which questions matter for this specific deal. It doesn’t chase hedge language, phrases like “it depends” or “I’m not sure”, where the real discovery signal lives.

The fix

AI for volume. A human for judgment on which one matters here, and for chasing the hedge.

Lesson learned

55% of sales orgs already use generic AI for tactical content like scripts and collateral, but only 31% trust it for strategic tasks like qualification, because general tools lack business context.14 Lesson: know which side of that line your task is on before you delegate it.

The move

This workstream feeds a demo. It does not score one. Use AI to widen the raw material, question sets, recipe variants, then run the finished demo through the Demo Coherence Index, a separate step, before it goes live.

WS-06 · Competitive Positioning

Why AI battlecards converge to the same three bullets.

A twenty-year CI veteran of Cisco, Juniper, and Polycom put the boundary plainly: “Automation isn’t about replacing human intelligence, it’s about enhancing it.”15

Where it helps

Desk research synthesis plus conversation-intelligence extraction from real sales calls, combined into a first-pass battlecard in roughly 30 minutes.16

Where it fails

Letting a general model draft differentiation from its own training data. It weighs everything equally, so it lands on the same generic points every other AI battlecard lands on.

The fix

Ground every draft in your own win-loss transcripts and call notes. A human owns final sign-off.

Lesson learned

“AI research tools can hallucinate specifics — pricing numbers, feature claims, customer names.”16 Lesson: every specific claim in an AI-assisted battlecard needs a named, checkable source before a rep repeats it in front of a buyer.

Klue and Crayon were both named Leaders, among several, not exclusively, in Gartner’s inaugural Magic Quadrant for Competitive and Market Intelligence Platforms. Klue was also a Strong Performer in The Forrester Wave™: Market And Competitive Intelligence Platforms, Q4 2024.17,18 A real, analyst-validated category. It operationalizes the grounding discipline above at scale. It doesn’t replace it.

The move

Before any AI-assisted battlecard ships, ask it, and ask yourself, the question a skeptical rep will ask: “What specific deal did we actually win or lose because of this claim?” If the honest answer is none, the claim is generic, not competitive intelligence.

Model Selection

Which AI, for what.

Each model below has a real, structural reason it might be the better tool for a specific job. Not a vague “model quality” claim, and not something that resets next release, but a genuine difference in what the model can access or was built to do. Where the evidence is vendor-sourced only, that is stated plainly. Where a real strength ships with a real risk, both are here.

Claude

Best forDrafting and revising one long document that has to stay internally consistent through many rounds of edits. A competitive positioning brief you revise after every new objection you hear in the field. A multi-section analyst briefing deck where the same three differentiators need to appear worded the same way on page two and page twelve.

Why this matters: paste in a fifty-page draft and ask for one section to change, and a model that loses track of your established terminology halfway through will quietly introduce a different word for the same feature. Nobody catches it until an analyst asks which one is correct.

Why, honestly: real, but self-reported. Anthropic’s own retesting shows major improvement in long-document retrieval after specific prompt structuring.19 Independent researchers track long-context reliability as a real, improving category20 without crowning a winner. Treat “Claude holds format better over long documents” as a working hypothesis worth testing on your own material, not a settled, independently verified fact.

Microsoft Copilot

Best forFinding what your own company already knows before you write anything new. Your last five competitive teardowns live in PowerPoint decks scattered across SharePoint. Your pricing history lives in a spreadsheet an account manager built two years ago. Your last three responses to this exact analyst question live in Word documents in someone else’s OneDrive.

Why, honestly: the most solidly confirmed advantage in this table. Copilot is architecturally grounded in Microsoft Graph, so it can search and read your organization’s permissioned PowerPoint, Word, Excel, Teams, and OneDrive content directly. A standalone ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini account cannot do this. It has no access path into your company’s files at all.21 A Microsoft-commissioned Forrester study found real return on this in practice. Attribute it as commissioned, not independent, but the underlying architecture is documented and independently verifiable.22

Gemini

Best forTwo different things, both genuine. First, analyzing a video someone else made: your own recorded demo, or a competitor’s product video pulled from YouTube, reading the visuals and the spoken narration together instead of treating them as separate inputs. Second, turning a long written spec or technical document into a quick narrated walkthrough your team can watch instead of read.

Why, honestly: the video-analysis capability is real and currently distinctive. Gemini processes video and audio as one input rather than running separate transcription and image passes, and can reference specific timestamps directly, which is a genuine advantage when you want to point at the exact moment a competitor’s demo makes a claim.23 Google’s NotebookLM can turn a set of source documents into a narrated slide presentation, useful for a fast internal explainer, though it produces narrated slides, not live product footage, so it is not a substitute for a real demo video.24 Google’s video generation model, Veo, makes short eight-second clips with real, documented use for social and ad content.25 It has not been tested here against the specific job of a narrated technical product walkthrough, so verify its dialogue and accuracy on your own material before you trust it for anything buyer-facing.

Grok

Best forThe first flag that something moved: a competitor’s stock, an acquisition rumor, an executive statement, a product announcement, surfaced from live X activity before it shows up anywhere else. Genuinely useful for competitive research and for scoping a demo to a company’s current situation: if you’re about to build a demo for a company that just got acquired, just had a leadership change, or just missed earnings, that context changes what the demo should emphasize.

Why, honestly: Grok is the only major model with native, first-party access to live X post streams, not a training-cutoff snapshot. That is a genuine structural difference from every other model in this table, and it is the right tool for noticing that something happened.

The caution has to be foregrounded, not buried. A Tow Center for Digital Journalism study found Grok’s citations were wrong roughly 94% of the time. Multiple outlets have separately documented specific fabricated claims.26 Grok is not the tool for finding out what actually happened, only that something did. Once it flags a signal, close the loop against a real source: the company’s own investor relations page, its actual SEC filings (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K for a material event like an acquisition), or its official press release. That combination, Grok to notice, primary filings to confirm, is what makes this useful for competitive intelligence rather than a rumor mill you repeat in front of a buyer.

ChatGPT

Best forSeeing what an actual buyer most likely sees when they ask an AI chatbot about your product, your category, or your competitor.

Why this matters: a growing practice called AI-visibility monitoring, sometimes called generative engine optimization, is now checking how AI chatbots answer questions about a company the same way marketers used to check Google rankings. ChatGPT is the single largest surface for that check. OpenAI reported 900 million weekly active users as of February 2026, a large increase over the prior year, and ChatGPT still holds the largest share of worldwide AI-chatbot traffic of any tool in this table, at roughly 54%, ahead of Gemini, Claude, and Copilot combined, though that share has been shrinking as competitors gain ground.33,34 If you want to know what a prospect sees before they ever talk to your sales team, test the question in ChatGPT specifically, not just whichever model your team happens to prefer.

This is real enough that Adobe paid roughly 1.9 billion dollars to acquire Semrush in November 2025, explicitly framed around this kind of AI-visibility monitoring.35 The practice has moved from a fringe idea to an institutionally validated one.

Demo Capture

Demo capture tools, in brief.

One distinction is worth understanding before you evaluate any of these. It is the same test Mike Moran uses to judge any demo: does the tool capture the real, live product, or a recreation of it? A tool that captures your real product’s actual behavior inherits that behavior’s truth, bugs included. A tool that recreates the interface inherits only what its builder remembered to recreate.

The spectrum. The further right, the more a bug in the demo is also a bug in the product, which is the point.

Auto-capture walkthrough tools

ExamplesArcade, Tango, Scribe, Supademo

Capture a real click-through of your actual product as a screenshot or short video sequence, then add narration, hotspots, or AI voiceover on top.27 Real capture, but usually static once recorded. One practitioner testing Tango found it worked only in Chrome and produced a character-encoding bug in PDF exports.28 A separate hands-on comparison of documentation tools found Tango’s automation shallower than the name implies: “There was basic automation, but you need to type out your guides and steps manually.”29 The category-wide failure mode is simple either way: screenshots go stale the moment the product ships a UI change, and nothing in the tool detects it for you.

Interactive sales-demo platforms

ExamplesStorylane, Walnut, Navattic, Reprise

Capture your real product at the DOM level, then let you edit and rebrand the result into a clickable demo. Closer to “the real thing, edited” than a pure mockup, but editability cuts both ways. The demo can quietly drift from what the live product actually does if it isn’t recaptured on a schedule, and presales teams report spending real time on exactly this: one industry report found teams spending as much as 21 days a year maintaining sandbox environments.30 Pricing is a real barrier for smaller teams: engineers on Hacker News pushed back on one platform’s roughly $8,400 a year cost, with one commenter saying he built a comparable demo tour himself in about ten minutes using open-source tools, and the vendor’s own co-founder confirmed the product is built for larger companies, not bootstrapped ones.31

Live-data-injection tools

ExamplesSaleo, Reprise Reveal

Closest to “the thing itself,” not a recreation of it. These inject editable, realistic data directly into your real, functional product during a live sales call, rather than cloning or recreating the interface.32

Most sources for this category are vendor-published: comparison blogs, press releases, and reviews, not independent journalism. Four tools above (Navattic, Tango, Scribe, Storylane) have real independent practitioner accounts cited directly. Walnut, Reprise, Arcade, Consensus, Saleo, and Supademo turned up vendor sources only in this pass. A dedicated, independently-sourced evaluation of this category is planned as a separate guide.

The Deliverable

Why this checklist is foundational.

Every workstream above describes a different way AI produces something that looks finished but isn’t actually verified. A wrong line in a rehearsed demo script gets caught in rehearsal. A wrong claim in an analyst briefing, a competitive battlecard, or a customer reference doesn’t get caught until it’s in front of the person who can hurt you most, and by then it can cost a deal or damage a relationship with an analyst for years. This checklist is the one habit underneath everything above. It catches the difference before it ships, regardless of which workstream or which AI model produced the draft.

Copy it. Run every AI-assisted claim through it before it ships.

Claim Verification Checklist · Technical Marketing

Claim Verification Checklist

Run before any AI-assisted claim ships · technicalmarketing.org

  1. Does this claim trace to a real source, such as a transcript, a spec, or a win-loss note, and not the model’s general knowledge?
  2. Has a human confirmed every number, name, and date before this ships?
  3. If this references a specific analyst, competitor, or customer by name, has someone who actually knows them checked it?
  4. If this was drawn from a licensed research report (Gartner, Forrester, IDC), does the license actually permit that use?
  5. Could this claim survive a skeptical buyer or analyst asking, directly, “how do you know that”?
  6. If the answer to five is no, is the claim cut, or is the evidence behind it strengthened, before this ships?
The Inside Track

Six things that surprised us most.

Read everything above and here is the digest. Every one of these is already sourced in the sections you just read. If you only remember six things from this guide, remember these.

  1. Licensed research has a legal wall, not just an accuracy risk. Feeding Gartner’s published research into an AI model to prep for that same firm’s briefing can violate Gartner’s own usage policy, not just risk a wrong answer.2

  2. Positioning has no published AI playbook yet. Not one of the field’s best-known specialists, Dunford, Raskin, Lochhead, has put out their own AI workflow. This craft is wide open.5,6

  3. The tool with the flashiest capability has the worst accuracy record here. Grok can tell you something happened today. An independent study found it is wrong about what actually happened roughly 94% of the time.26

  4. A $8,400-a-year platform got rebuilt in ten minutes. A team on Hacker News reconstructed about 70% of one demo platform’s functionality themselves with free, open-source tools. The vendor’s own co-founder confirmed the product simply isn’t built for teams that size.31

  5. The most-used AI tool is also the one worth testing your own positioning in. ChatGPT still holds the largest share of AI-chatbot traffic of any tool in this guide. Testing how it answers questions about your product shows you what a buyer is statistically most likely to see first.33,34

  6. The habit matters more than the tool. IBM’s AR team pre-populates roughly 95% of RFI responses with AI, then has a human check every single one before it ships. Every workstream in this guide converges on that same habit. The tool changes. The habit doesn’t.1

He rose to IBM Distinguished Engineer over thirty years at IBM. His advice on what stays the same is worth taking specifically because he has watched multiple technology waves promise to change everything, and has the patents, the court case, and the thirty-year career to know which parts of the work actually didn’t.

What Doesn’t Change

Mike Moran’s own advice, drawn from nearly fifty years on the same side of this line, is to pay attention to what stays the same rather than what changes. A practitioner can build a career on understanding what the market needs, how the offer truly differentiates, and how to say that plainly, because as the tooling changes, those three things do not.‡

Every finding above will partly age. The named tools will be superseded. The specific percentages will be revised by their next report. What should not age is the shape of the judgment underneath each one. AI compresses drafting and first-pass synthesis. It does not compress the editorial call about which claim survives contact with a skeptical audience. That call is still yours to make, every time.
