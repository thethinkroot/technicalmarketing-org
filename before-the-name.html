Article II.

Before the Name.

Old Work.

The Technical Marketer Before the Title.

A history of a discipline that was practiced for decades before it had a name. Six decades of the function being performed under twelve different titles, traced through primary and authoritative secondary sources.

Edited by Chad Corriveau, Founding Editor · May 25, 2026

01 · A note on scope

What this piece claims, and what it does not

The discipline of this history.

This article is the history of technical marketing. Where the function came from. What it inherited. Why the name arrived last.

The unit of analysis is the function, not the title. A title history is brittle: terminology has been contested for sixty years across industrial marketing, business marketing, technology marketing, technical marketing, technical product marketing, solutions marketing, evangelism, and developer relations. A function history is durable. The load-bearing claim of this piece is that the function of producing technically credible material for technical buyers has been continuously performed inside the U.S. computing and software industry since the 1950s, under successively different names.

The industry under examination is U.S. computing and software. Industrial marketing as practiced in chemicals, aerospace, and defense in the 1930s through 1950s is treated as parent context, not core. The geography is the United States, principally Silicon Valley and the East Coast computing belt. The temporal scope of the analytical claim runs from the late 1960s forward; the inheritance from earlier industrial marketing is acknowledged but not claimed as continuous with the modern discipline.

The evidence hierarchy is explicit. Federal classification documents and corporate filings rank highest. University oral histories and contemporaneous practitioner books and articles rank next. Trade press and secondary writing rank below those. Wikipedia and aggregator sources are starting points only. Every named date and person in this piece is traceable to a primary or authoritative secondary source listed in the footnotes.

The period boundaries are justified by structural change in the pressure on the function, never by chronology or fame. This piece is not a history of marketing, of Silicon Valley, of the individuals named within it, or a forecast of what the function will become.

02 · The thesis

The work came first

The function the discipline now calls technical marketing was already being performed inside U.S. computing companies in the 1950s. In-house publication groups at Remington Rand, IBM, Burroughs, and Sperry produced engineer-targeted brochures, demonstration scripts, and reference architectures for the early generation of commercial mainframes. The work was editorial in nature: someone decided which technical claims the company would put in front of which audience, in what order, with what supporting evidence. The artifacts that resulted were the legible output of that judgment.1

The function did not have a settled name. The people performing it were variously titled product writers, application engineers, sales engineers, technical illustrators, or had no role definition at all. The titles described the artifacts the person produced. None of them described the work that decided which artifact to produce. That work, the editorial judgment under commercial pressure, is what this piece tracks across the next seventy years.

The names changed. The substance did not.

03 · The inheritance

Two parents, one function

The modern discipline inherited from two older sources. The first is industrial marketing, formalized in the academic literature in the 1930s after the 1929 financial crash forced U.S. manufacturers to develop a more disciplined approach to reaching business buyers. John H. Frederick's 1934 text Industrial Marketing was an early major work in the field, codifying practices that producers of capital equipment, machine tools, and industrial chemicals had been using to reach engineering and procurement audiences.2 The discipline taught its practitioners how to write for technically literate buyers, how to construct comparative evidence, and how to translate engineering specifications into commercial argument.

The second parent is sales engineering, which the U.S. Department of Labor later codified as occupation 41-9031.00.3 The classification pairs engineering credentials with commercial work and lists Technical Marketing Engineer as a variant title under the same code. This is the federal government's formal acknowledgment that the two functions are taxonomically related: technical marketing inherits the credibility and the credentials of sales engineering, and applies them to the production of structured material rather than the conduct of live customer conversations.

Neither parent fully explains the modern discipline. Industrial marketing came out of academic business schools and was generic across industries; sales engineering came out of field organizations and was oriented to individual deals. The function that became technical marketing combined the editorial discipline of the first with the technical credibility of the second, and applied both inside a new kind of company: the post-1960s computing and software firm that needed to reach a technical buyer at scale.

Two parents. One inheritance. No name yet.

04 · Era I

The function internalized · 1967–1990

The defining structural change of the founding era is the migration of the function from external agencies into the product companies themselves. The agency model and the in-house model coexisted through the 1970s. By 1990, the in-house model had won, and the discipline had settled into the company-internal location it still occupies.

1967 · Regis McKenna joins National Semiconductor

The earliest documented anchor of the era is Regis McKenna's arrival at National Semiconductor in 1967. McKenna's own oral history, recorded for the Stanford Silicon Genesis project in August 1995, places him inside the company in this year and describes the work he was doing as the construction of the technical marketing function from the inside.4 This was not yet what the discipline would later call itself, but the substance of the work, deciding which technical claims to put in front of which audiences and producing the structured material to do it, is recognizable to any modern practitioner.

1970 · McKenna founds his agency in Palo Alto

Three years later, McKenna left National Semiconductor and founded Regis McKenna, Inc. in Palo Alto. The Computer History Museum documents the firm as one of the earliest specialist technology marketing agencies in the region, and the firm went on to advise Apple, Intel, Genentech, and dozens of the foundational Silicon Valley product companies.5 The agency model that McKenna built externalized the function for the duration of an engagement and then handed it back to the product company, which by the early 1980s was increasingly hiring people to keep doing the work after the agency engagement ended.

1981 · Dennis Carter joins Intel as marketing engineer

Carter's Purdue ECE Outstanding Alumni profile, recorded in 1995, documents his move from Intel engineering into a role explicitly titled marketing engineer in 1981.6 This is one of the earliest documented uses of an in-house engineer-marketer title at a major product company. Carter went on to architect the Intel Inside campaign in 1991, but the relevant fact for this history is the title itself in 1981: an engineer credentialed by the company, located inside the marketing organization, doing what the agency model had previously done from outside.

1983 · Mike Boich and Guy Kawasaki at Apple

Mike Boich began as Apple's software evangelist for the Macintosh launch in the early 1980s. He recruited Guy Kawasaki into the role in 1983, and the term evangelism entered the discipline's vocabulary as a name for the developer-facing variant of the work.7 The Computer History Museum's profile of Kawasaki and Kawasaki's own contemporaneous writing document the role and the title. What the role actually did, recruiting third-party developers, producing technical demonstrations, defending architectural claims under engineering scrutiny, is recognizable as a variant of the function the discipline now tracks under technical marketing.

The agency model and the internal model coexisted. The internal model won.

05 · Six decades on one line

The timeline

Three eras within scope, two centuries of inheritance behind them. The boundaries between eras are marked by structural changes in the pressure on the function, not by chronology or fame.

Figure 01. The four periods. Inheritance, treated as parent context only. Era I, the function moving from agency to in-house. Era II, the framework and the proliferation of titles. Era III, the consolidation under the single name.

06 · Era II

The function framed · 1991–2023

The defining event of the middle era is the publication of Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm by HarperBusiness in 1991.8 The book gave the discipline its first canonical practitioner framework: the technology adoption lifecycle, the chasm between early adopters and the early majority, the bowling-alley strategy, and the whole-product concept. The framework took hold across enterprise tech in the 1990s and structured the way the function thought about its work for the next thirty years.

The lineage of the framework is not incidental. Moore had been a partner at Regis McKenna, Inc., before he wrote the book. The framework is in direct descent from the agency work of Era I, formalized and made portable through the act of publication. The discipline's first canonical text was written by someone who had spent the previous decade inside the founding firm of the founding era.

Under the framework, the function institutionalized at the larger product companies in the 1990s and 2000s. Cisco established Technical Marketing Engineer as a job classification. The U.S. Department of Labor added Technical Marketing Engineer as a variant title under occupation 41-9031.00.9 The title proliferated: Technical Product Marketing Manager. Solutions Marketing Manager. Developer Advocate. Developer Relations. Technical Evangelist. Product Evangelist. By the late 2010s, the same function was being performed under at least twelve distinct titles across the discipline.

The titles fragmented because adjacent functions kept claiming the legible parts of the work. Product marketing claimed the launches and the positioning. Sales engineering claimed the live demos and the deal-level technical conversations. Developer relations claimed the third-party developer audience. Each adjacent function took the visible part of the work, and the editorial judgment that decided which artifact to produce, with what framing, for which audience, stayed put. It did not get a stable name because the discipline could not agree which title should hold it.

The clearest contemporaneous evidence that the function was still searching for its name as late as 2021 is Eric Hendricks's piece on the Red Hat Partner Connect blog in November 2021, titled "What is a technical marketing manager?"10 That a practicing technical marketing manager at one of the largest enterprise infrastructure vendors needed to ask the question publicly is the diagnostic fact of the era.

Shared framework. Fragmented name.

07 · One discipline, twelve names

The discipline-evolution map

The function never lost its center. The titles around it multiplied because adjacent functions kept claiming the legible parts of the work, while the editorial judgment that decided which artifact to produce stayed put under a different name in each company.

Figure 02. The discipline's evolution. Two parent disciplines flow into a single trunk in Era I. The trunk forks into five title branches across Era II. The branches reconverge under a single name in Era III. The function being performed is materially the same across all of it.

The twelve titles the function has been performed under, named for completeness: Industrial Marketer, Sales Engineer, Marketing Engineer, Technical Marketing Engineer, Technical Product Marketing Manager, Solutions Marketing Manager, Developer Advocate, Developer Relations, Product Evangelist, Technical Evangelist, Technical Content Engineer, and Technical Marketing Manager. The list is not exhaustive, and the boundaries between titles are negotiated company by company. The point of the list is that no single title held the function continuously across all of Era II.

08 · An artifact from the middle

The discipline in 2009

The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine capture of technicalmarketing.org from July 8, 2009, is a useful artifact of where the discipline sat in the middle of Era II.11 The domain hosted Citrix tutorials, Xen virtualization diagrams, Adobe Illustrator how-tos, and articles about NetScaler. The site mixed everything it could call technical, because the field had not yet decided what counted as the core work.

The 2009 capture is not a claim of editorial continuity with the present publication. This domain has hosted writing about the discipline under multiple editorial regimes over the years. The 2009 capture is included here as evidence of the era's fragmentation, not as the publication's lineage. The field's failure to agree on what the discipline covered is precisely what the 2009 archive shows. The current editorial regime began in 2026 and intends to publish under a clearer definition than the field had in 2009.

The same archive adds one more layer. Captures of this domain record a Technical Marketing Meetup operating in Silicon Valley in 2013, eight practitioners attempting to organize the discipline a decade before the field settled on its name. The February agenda spans competitive analysis, testbed tooling, content, SEO, and global market signal analysis. The May session records a presentation titled SEO Basics, delivered by Mike Moran, identified on the archived page as a leader in the field.12 The group was performing the same act this publication performs now. Naming the work. The record shows how early the attempt began.

Twelve names. No answer yet on what counted.

09 · Era III

The function named · 2024–

The defining structural change of the present era is AI compression. The Anthropic Economic Index reports of 2025 and 2026 documented the work activities most exposed to absorption by large language models: structured artifact production at the boundary of natural-language drafting and templated structure.13 McKinsey's January 2026 piece on judgment-intensive work argued, from the other direction, that the work least exposed to AI compression is editorial judgment under buyer pressure: the work of deciding which evidence matters at which moment in a specific evaluation.14

The two reports point at the same structural fact from opposite directions. The work of producing technical artifacts is being compressed. The work of editorial judgment about which artifact to produce, with what framing, for which audience, is not. That judgment is what the function had always been doing under twelve different names. The compression of the legible artifact production around the judgment is what made the judgment finally visible as the core of the work.

The consolidation under a single name follows from this. Adjacent functions, sales engineering, product marketing, developer relations, technical writing, are each contracting around the parts of their work that AI cannot do. Technical marketing is contracting the same way. The difference is that the part of the work that remains, the editorial judgment, was always more concentrated in technical marketing than in the adjacent functions, because the discipline had been doing nothing else under twelve names for sixty years.

The discipline did not contract under AI pressure. It clarified. The titles that previously fragmented the work converged on the title under which the editorial judgment had always been most concentrated. The naming, in 2024 and forward, is the structural moment the field finally agreed on what to call the thing it had been doing all along.

10 · The throughline

What stayed

Across three eras and twelve names, the function performed the same work. Editorial judgment about which technical evidence the company put in front of its most skeptical audiences, under commercial pressure. The form of the artifacts changed. The medium changed. The titles changed. The names of the companies changed. The substance of the work did not.

This is the load-bearing claim of the article. Every named anchor in this piece, from McKenna at National Semiconductor in 1967 through Hendricks at Red Hat in 2021, is doing a recognizable version of the same work. They are deciding which technical claim to put in front of which audience, in what order, with what supporting evidence, under commercial pressure. The artifacts they produce vary widely. The judgment that decides which artifact to produce is the same judgment every time.

The discipline's history is not the history of how the function got invented. The function was not invented. It is the history of how the function, having been continuously performed for sixty years under twelve different names, came to agree on what to call itself.

The work was always older than the name. The name has finally caught up.

Editor's Note

This is Article II of Volume 01. The piece exists because the discipline's history has been told piecemeal across a dozen blog posts, oral histories, and academic accounts that rarely speak to each other, and never as one continuous story. The expectation is that this account will be corrected, sharpened, and expanded by practitioners who lived through eras the editor did not.

Particular invitations for correction: the boundary between Era I and Era II in specific companies, the year Cisco first used Technical Marketing Engineer as a job classification, named practitioners from the 1990s and 2000s whose work this piece omits, and the question of whether the inheritance section gives industrial marketing too much or too little weight. Practitioners with first-hand evidence on any of these are invited to suggest revisions. The next published version will reflect the better answers.
