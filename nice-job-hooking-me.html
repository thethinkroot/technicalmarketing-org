Practitioner Profile · Volume 01

Nice Job Hooking Me.

A story about a practitioner Google never called.

A conversation with Mike Moran — Edison, New Jersey · Summer 2026

Conducted and edited by technicalmarketing.org

Video · 101 Seconds · Practitioner Profile

A 101-second introduction to the conversation below.

There is a line in technical marketing between what can be shown and what has been built. Between the demo and the picture of the demo. Between the patent and the press release about the patent. Between the technique that holds up in a federal court and the industry that never hears it happened.

Call it the truth layer.

The Wayback Machine shows a meetup. May 2013. This domain. A room of practitioners trying to name something the market had not named yet. One of the presenter names in that archive was Mike Moran.

Thirteen years later, when this publication launched on the same domain, he was the first call we made.

The first message back was seven words.

“Nice job hooking me, even if it was AI.”

That reflex does not develop in a classroom. It develops in nearly fifty years of rooms where the thing either ran or it did not. Moran led the team that built the first commercial linguistic search engine in 1989, nearly a decade before Google existed. He holds thirteen patents in search and retrieval technology. He built a search technique at SoloSegment that a federal court would later reveal Google had been calling their secret sauce, while publicly denying any such thing existed.

He spotted a LinkedIn message from a publication he had never heard of. He answered anyway.

That should tell you something.

What followed was three rounds of written exchange. The conversation below is drawn from those exchanges, edited for length and sequence. Nothing has been put in his mouth. The words are his. The wit, it turns out, is also his.

On the reflex that nearly fifty years of building things that actually have to work will give you.

Technicalmarketing.org

Your first response to my outreach was “nice job hooking me, even if it was AI.” Where does that reflex come from?

Moran

I was hired by IBM in 1978 and quickly realized that as soon as you understand the computers don’t work, the world becomes a less frustrating place. Software always starts out not doing what it is supposed to do and frequently is released into production with many problems. So once you have seen the sausage being made, you want to ensure that what you personally eat has been USDA inspected.

I am old enough to have seen corporate executives approve the first projects whose business case was developed on a spreadsheet because “the computer said this project would work.” Seeing that taught me to ask many questions when I didn’t understand something, which some people don’t like to do. They think it makes them look dumb. I decided I would rather look stupid than be stupid.

The sausage, for the record, has not changed. Neither has the reluctance to ask what is in it.

On the line between the demo and the thing. And what happens when AI makes the picture of the thing look exactly like the thing.

TM.org

You led the team that built the first commercial linguistic search engine in 1989. Today someone can open Figma on a Tuesday and have something that looks like a working product by Wednesday morning. What is actually lost when the demo stops being the thing and becomes a picture of the thing?

Moran

Sometimes, nothing is lost. If the point is to show a new kind of user interface, Figma is great. Spreadsheets are a good example. If we could have used Figma to demonstrate the first spreadsheet, that would have been extremely helpful because the interface was the innovation. Once you had the interface, you just needed the computer to do math, which was not hard.

But if you used Figma to demonstrate a prototype of ChatGPT, that wouldn’t have been helpful, because the interface was not the innovation. The innovation was all behind the glass. So in each case, you need to judge whether Figma actually demonstrates the innovation.

TM.org

AI tools can now spin up working environments in hours, not mockups. Does that change where you draw the line, or does it just move where the deception can hide?

Moran

I don’t think it changes where I draw the line. I have seen many cooked demos. Cooking them with AI just makes them more flexible. Instead of following a single script, you can ask the audience to throw something out as a task.

It’s still true that you can judge something from Figma if the UI is what’s new. If what it does behind the scenes is what’s new, that requires a lot of testing before it’s believable.

The script got flexible. The gap behind the glass did not move.

On building what Google was secretly building, not knowing it, patenting it first, and finding out in court. The strangest validation story in search technology.

TM.org

The SoloSegment story stopped me. You built a technique, patented it, and years later a federal court case revealed Google had called it their secret sauce. Most of the industry never heard it happened. How do you make sense of that?

Moran

That was a strange experience. Google apparently had been collecting user behavior since 2003 to make an educated guess about whether searchers found what they were looking for. Every searcher’s movement is a signal of success or failure. Did the searcher click on the result? Immediately hit the back button? Or scroll down at the speed of reading? Did they then enter another query? Was that a rephrasing of the first one or a new question?

If you know which search results lead to success for each query, that becomes your ranking algorithm. Google had been doing this for years, but not only did they not file a patent, they publicly denied doing any such thing.

When I developed the analytics for our SoloSegment website search engine product, I independently thought these ideas made sense and built a search engine that learned from the searchers. We were awarded a patent and only after that did it come out in court that Google considered this technique its most important secret. I was floored.

SoloSegment had a great search engine but the business failed during COVID when venture capital dried up. We still have the patent if anyone wants to implement the technique. It definitely worked.

The patent remains. In 2026, as LLMs race to surface the most useful results from the least friction, the technique Google once called its most important secret is sitting in a filing cabinet in New Jersey.

TM.org

You said had you known what Google was doing, you would never have attempted it. Is that specific to you or is there a broader principle?

Moran

Sometimes it’s great to be naive. Had I known this is what Google was doing I would have despaired that we’d need tons of money and tons of data to make it work. I’d have never attempted it.

I think sometimes experience can prevent you from attempting something because it seems too bold. In this case, had I known Google was doing this, I probably would have assumed there were multiple patents already filed, that it took dozens of engineers, and way more signal data than we would ever have from the searches on a single corporate website. So I think I would have lost my nerve.

There are two things a practitioner can know. Whether something is possible. And whether someone else is already doing it at scale. Mike Moran had the first. The second would have ended it before it started.

Moran

But I don’t think there is a case to be made for ignorance in general. Knowing more usually leads to better decisions and market ignorance has been the cause of death of most new products I have ever seen. In this case, knowledge might have stopped me from trying something. But usually ignorance stops you from succeeding at what you do try.

On what buyers can see now that they could not see before. And whether that transparency is good news for everyone or just for the practitioners whose work actually holds.

TM.org

You said today’s buyers have more ways to size you up, and that it is good for you. What specifically are they looking at that they could not before?

Moran

I think what’s changed is that buyers are more technically savvy than when I started consulting seventeen years ago. There are usually two kinds of personas in B2B marketing: the business domain buyer who cares what you do, and the technical specialist buyer who cares how you do it.

What I see is that the gap in knowledge between the technical specialist buyer and me is much narrower than it once was. When I explain how I do things today, they much more readily understand it and it makes sense to them. They are buying me less for my reputation and testimonials than because they believe in the approach.

TM.org

Is that transparency good for everyone, or only for practitioners whose work actually holds up under scrutiny?

Moran

That change is good for clients because they make better purchasing decisions. But it’s also good for any consultant that can explain what they do in simple terms.

The ones who built their credibility on reputation rather than proof are now in a room where the buyer can read the difference. That room keeps getting better lit.

On nearly fifty years inside AI before it was a job title. And what the view from that distance actually looks like.

TM.org

You have been working in AI for longer than most of the people currently writing about it have been alive. From that vantage point, what do you actually see when you look at what AI is doing to the go-to-market stack right now?

Moran

It is true that I am seriously old. Nothing I was working on in the 1980s would be considered AI today because the real definition of AI is whatever a computer can do that surprises us.

I think marketers should listen to the advice from Jeff Bezos to pay attention to what stays the same more than to what changes. Similarly, marketers can build a career around what won’t change: they will always need to understand what their market needs, understand how they truly differentiate from their competitors, and craft a message that communicates that advantage. As technology changes, those things don’t change.

If your product is now being purchased by AI agents, you need to understand them and craft a message for them. Or you need such a strong brand image that the human buyer specifies your brand in the prompt.

TM.org

Where is the truth layer getting thinner, and where does it hold?

Moran

AI is great at going deep on a task, but it is not very good at going wide. The broader your knowledge, the more dots you can connect and the better you can use AI to do the detailed work. I think that human beings will keep finding ways to remain valuable by broadening their perspective.

The man who has been building search technology since 1978 is not worried about AI. Make of that what you will.

On Bezos, the baseline, and the enterprise software platforms now learning what happens when simplicity was always the product and you spent twenty years ignoring it.

TM.org

You cited Bezos on what stays the same. I would argue Amazon also shifted what customers believed they were entitled to expect. Bezos did not just serve existing needs. He moved the baseline. Does your framework account for that?

Moran

I still think it is very stable. Customers always expect more once their initial needs are fulfilled, but the stability stems from their expectations moving along the same tried and true path of selection, price, and delivery. Knowing what they always want allows you to consistently make decisions about what to do next through that lens.

For example, more than twenty years into Amazon’s history, it introduced a feature to delay deliveries for a lower price because it had twenty years worth of things to do before it needed to start trading off one need versus the other.

TM.org

Enterprise software customers are leaving platforms they have used for years. Not because their needs changed. Because AI is threatening to serve the same needs without the platform. Is that Bezos proving right or proving wrong?

Moran

I think they are leaving because LLMs better serve a need than they do. The need for simplicity. Those platforms work but no one likes using them. I think if people liked using them they’d stay with them. But they like using a chatbot better.

Bezos was right. The platforms just assumed they were the ones who got to deliver on it.

This profile began with a LinkedIn message and a seven-word response that told us everything we needed to know.

What followed, over three rounds of exchange, was a practitioner who has spent nearly fifty years on the same side of the same line. The side where the thing either works or it does not. Where the patent either holds in court or it does not. Where the buyer either understands the approach and believes it, or they do not.

The truth layer is not a framework Moran named. It is a condition he has operated inside for long enough that he no longer distinguishes it from the work itself.

That is what this publication is for. The practitioners for whom the truth layer is not an idea.

His closing view on what does not change across technology waves does not stay contained to this page — it closes out the AI field guide too, same conversation, different deliverable.

It is the job.

Mike Moran

Author of Do It Wrong Quickly, co-author of Search Engine Marketing Inc. and Outside-In Marketing, founder of Biznology, and co-founder and Chief Product Officer of ThinkStream. He rose to IBM Distinguished Engineer during his thirty years at IBM and was awarded thirteen patents in search and retrieval technology.
